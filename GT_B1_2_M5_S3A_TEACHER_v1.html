§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

Fake News — How Do You Know What’s Real?

★ GT ORIGINAL — TEACHER ACTIVITY: 3 Headlines

Before showing the interactive headline game below, write these 3 headlines on a whiteboard or share them verbally. Ask students to debate which is real, partially fake, or completely invented. Use modal deduction: “This must be real because… / This can’t be true because… / This might be partially edited because…”

📰 “Study finds people who drink coffee live longer than those who don’t.”
📰 “Scientists confirm 5G towers cause brain damage — governments are hiding the report.”
📰 “WhatsApp will begin charging users €3 per month starting January 2026.”

Answers: (1) Partially real — some studies show correlation, not causation. (2) Completely fake. (3) Recurring urban myth — always false, always believed.

⚡ HEADLINE GAME — Real, Partial, or Fake?

Click your verdict for each headline. Justify your answer using must / can’t / might.

HEADLINE 1
“Mexico becomes world’s largest avocado exporter, surpassing all other countries combined.”
✅ Real — Mexico produces approximately 30% of the world’s avocados and is the largest exporter. The phrasing is slightly misleading (“all others combined”) but broadly accurate.
HEADLINE 2
“New study: reading on screens causes permanent vision loss in 80% of users over 30.”
❌ Fake — No such study exists. Screen use can cause eye strain, not permanent vision loss. The “80%” statistic is invented. Classic fear-based misinformation structure.
HEADLINE 3
“Electric vehicle sales in Mexico grow 40% in 2024 — but only 0.3% of total market.”
✅ Real — This reflects actual Mexican EV market data. High growth percentage + very small base = both true simultaneously. This is how statistics are used selectively in media.
Q1  Personal
Have you ever believed something that later turned out to be false? What was it, and how did you find out it was wrong?
Q2  Work
Has fake news or misinformation ever affected your company, your industry, or a supplier? What might the consequences have been?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Look at Headline 2 again. Say out loud: “That can’t be true because… A real study would have… They must be trying to…” — Three modal deductions in 30 seconds. That’s critical thinking in English.
GT Original for S3A: 3 headlines as a physical activity first, THEN the interactive game reinforces it digitally. The teacher’s job is to make the debate emotional: “Would you share Headline 1 with your family on WhatsApp? What about Headline 2?” The gap between “I wouldn’t share that” and “my uncle already did” drives authentic production. Modal deduction is the perfect grammar tool here: we reason about information we cannot fully verify.
WhatsApp is the primary fake news vector in Mexico. Almost every learner in a Bajío corporate context has a family group chat where misinformation spreads. Naming this specifically (without judgement) generates immediate personal engagement. Headline 3 (EV data) is the most professionally relevant — use it with engineering and logistics learners.
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“If algorithms decide what news you see, can you ever trust your own opinion?”

The average person encounters between 4,000 and 10,000 pieces of information per day — most of them on a phone screen. Algorithms decide what appears in your feed based on what keeps you engaged, not what is true. This creates echo chambers: you see content that confirms what you already believe. Fake news doesn’t have to be believed by everyone — it just has to be shared often enough to shape perception.

The topic statement uses an indirect conditional to pose an epistemological problem — “can you trust your own opinion?” is not rhetorical. After 90 seconds of discussion, bridge to grammar: “When you say ‘I don’t know if this is real,’ the English tools for that uncertainty are must / can’t / might.”

§4   Colloquial  ·  5 min

M5 Phrases — In Fake News Context

“It went viral.”
Spread rapidly and massively
“The fake vaccine story went viral before any fact-check could reach the same audience.”
“They’re clout-chasing.”
Seeking attention at any cost
“Some media outlets share fake news because they’re clout-chasing — engagement matters more than accuracy.”
“No cap.”
I’m being completely serious
“No cap — I fact-checked that article and every single “statistic” in it was invented.”
“Shade was thrown.”
Subtle indirect criticism
“The newspaper said ‘sources close to the government claim…’ — shade was thrown without accountability.”
The connection between “shade was thrown” and anonymous sourcing in journalism is a nuanced one — it shows how colloquial language can describe editorial tactics. If learners make this connection independently, acknowledge it: “Exactly — anonymous sources can be a form of institutional shade-throwing.” This kind of connection-making is what B1.2 fluency looks like.
§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Modal Deduction — Applied to Media & Information

⚠ REAL SITUATION
Someone in your team shares a WhatsApp message: “Scientists say coffee causes cancer in 90% of cases — stop drinking it immediately.” No source, no date. You want to reason out loud about whether it’s true before responding. How?
✎ MODAL DEDUCTION IN CRITICAL THINKING CONTEXTS
What you’re doingStructureExample
Near-certain positivemust beThis must be misinformation — no credible study would phrase it that way.
Near-certain negativecan’t beThat claim can’t be accurate — it contradicts years of established research.
Expressing uncertaintymight beThe headline might be technically true but the context could be misleading.
Past positive deductionmust haveThe article must have been edited after publication — the original version said something different.
Past negative deductioncan’t haveThe journalist can’t have verified this — there are no named sources anywhere.
⚠ Common error: “It must to be fake.” → WRONG. Never use “to” after a modal verb.

Carlos Mendez on Information & Trust

OPINION METER — Before you listen:
“Most people can’t reliably identify fake news — including highly educated professionals.”
👤 Strongly Disagree👥👥 Strongly Agree
50%
Carlos Mendez — B1.2 M5 S3A — ElevenLabs speed 0.85
0:001:50

Carlos Mendez:

I run a critical thinking workshop for senior managers. In every session, I show them three pieces of information and ask: which is real? And every single time, at least one highly educated executive gets it wrong. Every time.

The problem must be deeper than intelligence or education. Research in cognitive psychology suggests that we don’t evaluate information based on truth — we evaluate it based on how it makes us feel. Information that confirms what we already believe feels credible. Information that challenges us might feel threatening, even when it’s accurate.

Think about this: if you read a headline that supports your political view, how long do you spend checking the source? Thirty seconds? None at all? Now compare that to a headline that contradicts your view. The journalist can’t have been wrong — you’ll check everything twice.

This is what algorithms exploit. They must have been designed to understand that emotional engagement beats informational accuracy every time. So the question is not “is this fake news?” The question is: “Why does this feel true to me? And is that a reason to trust it — or a reason to be suspicious?”

Carlos’s final question — “Why does this feel true to me? And is that a reason to trust it — or a reason to be suspicious?” — is the takeaway of the session. After comprehension, ask learners to think of one thing they currently believe strongly. Ask: “Is it possible you believe it partly because it feels emotionally right?” Do not push for answers — let the question sit. This level of metacognitive awareness is exactly what B1.2 philosophical discussion requires.
§SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

Think Out Loud

RECALL
What does Carlos say about the relationship between emotion and credibility? Paraphrase his argument using “he must be suggesting that…”
Start with: “He must be saying that when information confirms our beliefs, we…”
DEDUCTION
Your colleague shares a news article without a byline, published on a website you’ve never heard of. Make 3 deductions about its reliability using must / can’t / might.
Use vocabulary: source, credibility, fact-check, verify
WORK CONTEXT
How might fake news or misinformation affect your industry specifically? Think about supply chain rumours, competitor information, or regulatory news.
“If a competitor spread misinformation about our product, it might… / We can’t afford to ignore…”
DEBATE
Is it the user’s responsibility to fact-check, or should platforms and governments do it for us? Argue your position.
Use “No cap” or “it went viral” to anchor your argument with M5 language
PERSONAL
Think of one thing you believe strongly. Applying Carlos’s logic: why do you believe it? Is it possible the algorithm has shaped your view on this?
This is about honesty, not politics. Use “I might be…” to stay open
CHALLENGE
Create 2 fake headlines about a company in the Bajío region that sound believable. Then explain what must be missing from each one that a critical reader would catch.
Use vocabulary: source, bias, credibility, context — apply the criteria you learned today
Q6 (create fake headlines) is the most creative and analytically demanding activity. Learners must understand what makes misinformation believable in order to construct it — this reversal forces deep comprehension of the vocabulary and concepts. Q5 (personal question about beliefs) requires psychological safety — model it with yourself first: “I’ll be honest — I might have an echo chamber around [topic]. I realised it when…”
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“Let me verify that before we act on it.”
Use when a colleague presents surprising data or news. Professional, non-accusatory. Signals analytical rigour without questioning the person’s integrity.
“That can’t be the full picture — what’s the source?”
Use in meetings when decisions are being made on partial information. Combines modal deduction with a practical follow-up question. This phrase stops bad decisions.
“We might be in an echo chamber on this.”
Use in strategy sessions when the group has been agreeing too easily. Shows leadership awareness and intellectual humility. Rare in corporate Mexico — powerful when used.
