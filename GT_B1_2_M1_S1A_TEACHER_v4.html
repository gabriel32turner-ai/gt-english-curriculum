§ 01
Warm-Up — This or That
Instant opinions · No filtering allowed · Get the debate started immediately
5–7 min

Haz clic en tu respuesta — luego justifícala con una oración PPC.

This
First impressions are usually accurate — your gut reading of someone in three seconds is information, not prejudice.
VS
That
First impressions are mostly bias — they tell you more about the observer than about the person being observed.
This
You can deliberately shape the impression you make — presence, tone, body language can all be trained and projected.
VS
That
The harder you try to "project" a certain impression, the more performative and inauthentic you look — authenticity can't be faked.
🎯 Teacher: After each pick, demand a PPC justification: "Tell me why — using 'I've been thinking that…' or 'I've been noticing in my workplace that…'". Don't accept opinions without a PPC attempt. This is the grammar hook for §4.
Capa 1 · Pedagogical
  • Pair 1 is the cognitive frame for the whole session. Push students past the easy answer — first impressions can be both information AND bias. Make them choose anyway.
  • Pair 2 is the tension between authenticity (§3 flip card) and projection (§2 verb "project"). They will return to this in §5 reading.
  • PPC requirement after each: "I've been thinking…", "I've been noticing…". Log errors for §4 grammar correction.
Capa 2 · Engagement
  • "In 10 seconds — which side? No explanation. Just point." Then: "Now defend it in 45 seconds. I'm timing."
Capa 3 · Growth & Retention
  • "By the end of this session, you'll have the exact language to talk about presence, credibility, and how impressions evolve at work — without reaching for Spanish."

Capa 1 · Pedagogical
  • All verb examples use PPC in workplace/identity context. After vocab: "Which verb describes what you've been doing in your first weeks at your current job? One PPC sentence."
  • "project" — students confuse with "predict." Project = communicate outwardly (a quality). "She's been projecting confidence" ≠ "She's been predicting confidence."
  • "convey" vs "project" — convey is about meaning/message; project is about quality/feeling. Both with PPC: "He's been conveying urgency / projecting calm."
  • "read (someone)" — slang-adjacent but B1.2 essential. "He's been reading the room perfectly." Practice in §5 listening.

§ 04
Grammar — Present Perfect Continuous (Introduction)
The tense for ongoing professional identity · "She has been building her credibility…"
12–14 min
The rule: Use Present Perfect Continuous (PPC) for an action that started in the past, has been continuing, and is still relevant now. Especially for ongoing professional behavior, habits, and impressions. Form: subject + have/has + been + verb-ing.
+
Affirmative
She has been projecting confidence since her first week.
−
Negative
He hasn't been conveying urgency in the recent meetings.
?
Question
How long have you been working on your executive presence?
!
When to use
Ongoing action, visible now: "She's been establishing her authority for months."
✓ Present Perfect Continuous
Ongoing & visible now: "She has been building her credibility."
Duration matters: "He's been projecting authority for weeks."
Still happening: "Trust has been growing across the team."
With "since/for/lately": "I've been noticing his presence lately."
✗ Simple Past (NOT here)
Finished event: "She built her credibility." (and stopped)
Specific moment: "He projected authority at the meeting."
Completed: "Trust grew." (one specific past growth, done)
With "yesterday/in 2019": "I noticed his presence yesterday."
⚠️ Spanish-Speaker Errors to Stop NOW
✗ She is building her credibility since January.→✓ She has been building her credibility since January.
✗ I am working on my presence for months.→✓ I have been working on my presence for months.
✗ He has projecting authority lately.→✓ He has been projecting authority lately.
✗ How long are you working here?→✓ How long have you been working here?
Activity A · Multiple Choice — PPC Form

Choose the grammatically correct option.

1. Since the merger, the team _____ trust in the new leadership.
2. How long _____ on your executive presence?
3. He _____ enough professionalism in client meetings recently.
Activity B · True / False — PPC Uses

Decide whether each statement about PPC is True or False.

1. PPC is used for actions that started in the past and are still continuing or recently stopped with visible results.
2. "She has been projecting confidence yesterday at 4pm" is a correct PPC sentence.
3. We use PPC with time markers like since, for, lately, recently, all morning.
4. PPC is the most natural tense in English for describing an ongoing professional reputation or evolving workplace dynamic.
Activity C · Reorder — Build the PPC Sentence

Drag the chunks into the correct order to form a natural PPC sentence.

  • ⠿since the restructure

Capa 1 · Pedagogical
  • This is the FIRST formal exposure to PPC in B1.2. They've been hearing it all session — now name it. Form first, use second, contrast third.
  • Spanish interference: "estoy trabajando desde…" → "I am working since…" is the #1 error. Drill until reflexive: "since/for + how long + PPC."
  • Contrast PPC vs Simple Past matters at B1.2: Simple Past is finished, PPC is ongoing. "She built credibility" (and then?) vs "She has been building credibility" (still ongoing).
  • After Activity A: ask each student to PRODUCE one PPC sentence about themselves at work. Right now. No scaffolding.
Capa 2 · Engagement
  • "This tense — Present Perfect Continuous — is what makes you sound like a senior English speaker. Not 'I work here for 5 years.' But 'I've been working here for 5 years.' Hear the difference?"
Capa 3 · Growth & Retention
  • "This is the tense we'll be using in EVERY session of M1. By S5, PPC should be automatic — like a reflex."
§ 05
Reading — "The Three-Second Mistake"
An operations manager describes the bias she's been correcting for two years
10–12 min
🎯 Key vocab to spot: PPC structures · colloquial phrases — circle every PPC use you find while reading. Target: 8+.
Sarah Chen
Operations Manager · 11 yrs in industry · LinkedIn essay
📖 Reading

I've been in this industry for eleven years. I've hired more than forty people. And I've been making the same mistake for most of that time — letting the first three seconds of a meeting determine how I listen to the next sixty minutes.

We talk about unconscious bias in training sessions. We nod, sign the form. But I've been discovering something uncomfortable over the last two years of actually tracking my own judgments: the three-second impression doesn't just affect how I feel about someone — it affects how I listen. If my first impression is negative, I've been collecting evidence to confirm it. If it's positive, I've been filtering out the things that should concern me.

Last year, I almost passed on the best hire I've ever made. She walked in looking exhausted — Friday afternoon, she'd been traveling all week, and her first words were an apology for being five minutes late. My instant read: disorganized, unreliable, not managing herself well. I'd been building that case in my head before she'd finished her first sentence.

Then she pulled out a printed agenda for our conversation. She'd been preparing for three days — researching our processes, identifying inefficiencies, drafting a 90-day plan. She spoke for forty minutes and I interrupted her twice — both times to ask her to slow down because the information was too good to miss. Honestly though, by minute fifteen, I knew I'd almost let her walk out the door.

She's been leading our operations division for eight months. Revenue efficiency has been increasing 23% quarter on quarter since she took over.

The three-second impression has been one of the most studied phenomena in social psychology. Research has been consistently showing that humans form stable opinions within milliseconds — and then spend the rest of the interaction confirming what they already decided. The technical term is "anchoring bias." I'm not gonna lie — I've been calling it "the three-second mistake" because that's what it is. A mistake with consequences that have been compounding over time.

I've been running a simple exercise with my team since January: after every first meeting, write down your immediate impression. Then, six weeks later, read it back. What has been changing? What has been staying the same? The exercise has been revealing more about the observer than the observed.

We've been learning to distrust our first impressions — not because they're always wrong, but because they've been deciding too much.

Activity A · Reading Comprehension — Multiple Choice

Choose the best answer based on the text.

1. According to Sarah, what is the main consequence of a negative first impression?
2. What changed Sarah's mind about the candidate who arrived "exhausted"?
3. What does Sarah's six-week exercise reveal?
Activity B · Reading Comprehension — True / False

Based on the article only.

1. Sarah has been correcting her first-impression bias for eleven years.
2. The exhausted candidate had been preparing for the interview for three days.
3. Sarah's argument is that first impressions are always wrong and should be ignored.
4. Revenue efficiency has been increasing under the new operations leader.
Capa 1 · Pedagogical
  • Have students circle every PPC structure on first read. Count: 11+. This is the high-density input pre-grammar consolidation.
  • After Q3, push for personal application: "Has any first impression in your professional life been deciding too much for you?"
  • Term to teach: "anchoring bias" — the first piece of information disproportionately influences all subsequent judgments. Useful in any HR/leadership conversation.
Capa 2 · Engagement

Capa 3 · Growth & Retention

Listening — "Wait, I Think I Was Wrong"
Two colleagues compare notes on a new hire — and revise their first impression in real time
10–12 min
🎧
Wait, I Think I Was Wrong
2 voices · ~2:40 · Jamie (PM) & Marcus (colleague)

🎵 Audio placeholder — ElevenLabs Sarah + Michael · Rate 0.87 · Stability 0.60
Generate from script below and replace this block with the audio embed.

▶ Show ElevenLabs Script

JAMIE: Marcus — can I ask you something?

MARCUS: Of course. What's up?

JAMIE: It's about the new person. Elena.

MARCUS: What about her?

JAMIE: I've been thinking about her since the first week. Honestly — I didn't think she was going to last.

MARCUS: Really? Why not?

JAMIE: I'm not gonna lie — she barely said anything in the first three meetings. I was watching her and just… nothing. I thought, this person isn't engaged. She hasn't been contributing.

MARCUS: Hmm. And now?

JAMIE: I've been watching her work for the past month. She's been building the entire tracking system that I asked for in week one — and nobody told her how. She just did it. Three weeks of silent work, and it's better than anything our team has been producing for the past year.

MARCUS: That's such a mood. I went through the same thing with her. Week one — I thought, she seems… I don't know, distant?

JAMIE: Exactly. And now?

MARCUS: Honestly though — I think I've been completely wrong about her. Her approachability has been there all along — I just wasn't looking in the right way.

JAMIE: What changed for you?

MARCUS: She stopped me in the corridor last week and spent fifteen minutes explaining a risk I'd been missing on the client project. She'd been tracking it for two weeks without saying anything — because she was still assessing the situation.

JAMIE: So the silence has been… strategy.

MARCUS: More like… professionalism. She has been reading the environment, establishing trust. We've been judging presence by volume. That's a bit much, isn't it?

JAMIE: Fair enough. I've been meaning to say something to her. To acknowledge it.

MARCUS: She'd appreciate that. She's been wondering, I think. Whether people are seeing her.

JAMIE: We have been — just too slowly.

Voices: Sarah (Jamie) + Michael (Marcus) · Rate 0.87 · Stability 0.60 · Style 0.20
