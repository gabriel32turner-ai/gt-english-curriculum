Is University Still Worth It?

Q1  GT Original
Hands up if you have a university degree. Now — keep your hand up if you use it daily in your current job. Look at the gap. What does that tell you?
Q2  Personal
If you had to choose today — knowing what you know now — would you take the same degree again? Or would you do something completely different?
Q3  Opinion
Think of someone you know who is very successful without a degree. What did they have that formal education can’t easily give you?
Q4  Work
If your company posted a job tomorrow and two candidates applied — one with 5 years of experience, one with a master’s degree but no experience — who should they hire? Why?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Complete this sentence out loud: “Although I studied [subject], I ended up working in [field]. As a result, I…” — You just used two linking words. That’s today’s grammar in action.
GT Original for M6 S1: the hand-raise gap is the session’s emotional hook. For corporate learners who are already working — many with degrees they “don’t use” — this question is deeply personal. Don’t rush it. The silence after the gap is as valuable as the discussion. Q4 (experience vs. degree) is the most professionally relevant for managers who hire: let them debate genuinely, as this is a decision they actually make. The Quick Win introduces linking words naturally before grammar is taught.
This is the opening session of M6 — the final module of B1.2. Frame it as a milestone: “You’ve been learning English to have real conversations about real things. This module is the most personal one yet. Today’s topic is the kind of thing you debate at dinner, not just in class.”
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“A university degree used to be the answer to every career question. Whereas before it opened every door, today it might not even get you an interview.”

In Mexico, university enrolment has risen consistently for 30 years. Despite this, youth unemployment among graduates runs higher than among non-graduates in some sectors. Global companies like Google, Apple, and Salesforce no longer require degrees for many roles. Even though a degree still signals discipline and commitment, employers increasingly value portfolios, certifications, and demonstrated skills. The question is not whether university is good — it’s whether it’s still worth the cost, the time, and the opportunity lost.

The topic text uses linking words deliberately: “Whereas before,” “Despite this,” “Even though.” After 90 seconds of pair discussion, draw attention to them: “Notice how those linking words made the argument feel more intelligent? Today we’re going to make your arguments sound like this.”

§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Linking Words & Discourse Markers

⚠ REAL SITUATION
You’re explaining to a colleague why you didn’t take a promotion last year. You want to give a structured, intelligent answer — not just a list of reasons. You need words that connect your ideas and show the relationship between them. That’s what linking words do.
✎ LINKING WORDS & DISCOURSE MARKERS — FULL SYSTEM
These words don’t add information — they show how your ideas relate. They make you sound organised and intelligent.
FunctionWordsExampleNote
Contrastalthough / even though / whereas / despite / howeverAlthough I studied engineering, I ended up in sales.although + subject + verb. despite + -ing (no subject)
Resultas a result / therefore / consequentlyAs a result, I decided to change career direction.Usually at start of new sentence or clause
Additionfurthermore / in addition / besidesFurthermore, the degree opened unexpected doors.Used to build an argument, not list
Concessionhowever / nevertheless / even soThe costs were high. However, it was worth it.Between two contrasting ideas; full stop before
⚠ Critical error: “Despite she worked hard” → WRONG. “Despite working hard” → CORRECT. Despite must be followed by a noun or gerund (-ing), never a subject + verb.

Carlos Mendez on Education & Career

OPINION METER — Before you listen:
“A university degree is still the most reliable path to a successful career in Mexico — despite the evidence that suggests otherwise.”
👤 Strongly Disagree👥👥 Strongly Agree

Carlos Mendez — B1.2 M6 S1A — ElevenLabs speed 0.85
0:001:50

Carlos Mendez:

I coach senior executives. Many of them have MBAs from the best universities in the country. Although their credentials are impressive, the skills they use every day — communication, emotional intelligence, decision-making under pressure — were not taught in any classroom.

That doesn’t mean university is worthless. Furthermore, a degree still signals something real: discipline, persistence, the ability to complete what you start. Employers value that signal — even when the content is irrelevant.

But here is what I tell young people who ask me: whereas before, a degree was the door, today it’s just the key to the lobby. You still have to get to the right floor on your own.

Despite spending four years in university, most of my clients say they learned more in their first year of work than in all four years of study. That’s not an argument against university. It’s an argument for what comes after it — and how quickly you adapt.

So: is it worth it? As a result of everything I’ve observed over 20 years of coaching, my answer is: it depends entirely on what you do with the time on either side of it.

Carlos’s script uses all four types of linking words: although (contrast), furthermore (addition), whereas (contrast), despite (contrast with gerund — model the correct form), as a result (result). After comprehension, point to “despite spending four years” — this is the grammar error prevention in context. §SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

Your Education, Your Arguments

RECALL
Carlos says a degree is “the key to the lobby — not the room.” What does he mean? Explain in 2 sentences using a linking word.
Start: “Although a degree opens initial doors, it doesn’t…”
PERSONAL
Looking at your own career: what skills do you use most at work that your education gave you? And what skills did you have to learn on the job, despite your qualification?
Use: despite + -ing. Demonstrate the correct form.
DEBATE
Your company is hiring. Two candidates: one with a degree and 1 year experience, one without a degree and 4 years experience. Build the argument for EACH one. Use linking words to structure both cases.
Use: whereas, although, furthermore, however — one per argument
WORK CONTEXT
If your company stopped requiring degrees for new hires, what would change? Although it might widen the talent pool, what might be lost?
Build a structured argument for and against. Use as a result / therefore for consequences.
CHALLENGE
Design your ideal 4-year programme that replaces a traditional university degree. What must it include? What can you skip? Use linking words to justify each choice.
This is the most creative question — use furthermore and in addition to build your curriculum
COLLOQUIAL
Use all 4 M6 phrases in a short monologue about your own career path: “It’s a game-changer,” “I’m figuring it out,” “That’s the goal,” “I’ve come a long way.” Make it real.
60 seconds, personal, genuine — connect each phrase with a linking word
Q6 (all 4 M6 phrases in a monologue) is the most emotionally resonant for learners who have been with you through the level. “I’ve come a long way” lands with real weight when it’s true. Allow silence. Q5 (alternative curriculum) is the highest creativity question and produces the most natural use of addition markers (furthermore, in addition). For corporate learners, ask: “What would you add to your current team’s onboarding programme if you could?”
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“Although we have the credentials, we don’t have the experience yet.”
Use in a team meeting when managing expectations. Signals self-awareness and intellectual honesty. Works when pitching a new project your team hasn’t done before.
“As a result, we need to reconsider our approach.”
Use after presenting a problem or unexpected outcome. Signals structured thinking and leads naturally into a solution. One of the most useful sentence starters in professional English.
“It’s been a game-changer for the team.”
Use when describing a new process, tool, or person that has significantly improved how your team works. Enthusiastic but measured. Works in reports, presentations, and casual conversation.

Rule: every argument must include at least 2 different linking words. Partner challenge: “Your weakest link is…”
The partner challenge (“your weakest link is…”) forces analytical listening. Learners must evaluate the argument structure, not just the content. This is authentic B1.2 discourse. The 6 positions cover the full spectrum from “university is always worth it” to “it should be abolished” — no two learners should have identical positions.
