§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

Reality TV — Why Are We So Obsessed?

Q1  GT Original
Quick poll: raise your hand if you watch reality TV. Now — keep your hand up if you’d admit it publicly to your manager. Why the difference?
Q2  Opinion
Have you ever felt genuinely sad, angry, or excited about someone on a reality show — a person you have never actually met? What does that tell you about how these shows work?
Q3  Critical
Reality TV is edited. Producers decide what you see. Contestants sign contracts before filming. Can any of it actually be “real”? What must they be hiding?
Q4  Debate
If you had to name one thing that makes reality TV different from a scripted show, what would it be? And is that thing actually “real”?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Think of any reality show. Say this out loud: “The producers must be editing out all the boring parts. The contestants might feel…” — You’re already making deductions like a native speaker.
Q1 is the GT Original for M5 S2: the gap between what people say (“I don’t watch reality TV”) and what they do is the speaking topic. Make it visible before content begins. Most corporate learners watch something — cooking shows, talent shows, dating shows. Q3 explicitly previews the grammar: “what must they be hiding?” is natural modal deduction. Don’t flag it yet — just let it land.
This session has very high personal engagement potential because almost everyone has a relationship with this type of content — even if it’s “I hate it.” That position generates just as much language as “I love it.” Create two sides at the start and let them debate. Both sides drive production.
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“Reality TV must be doing something right — we keep watching even when we know it’s manipulated. Why?”

Reality TV is the most-watched genre on global streaming platforms. Shows like Big Brother, Survivor, Love Island, La Academia, and MasterChef have audiences in the hundreds of millions. They are produced by large teams, heavily edited, and carefully designed to trigger emotional responses. Yet we watch them and feel that what we’re seeing is real. The paradox is not accidental — it is the product.

The speaking topic opens with a modal deduction statement: “must be doing something right.” This is intentional — model deduction language appears in the topic itself. After 90 seconds of pair discussion, ask: “Notice how I started the question with ‘must be’ — that’s today’s grammar. You were already using it naturally.”

§4   Colloquial  ·  5 min

M5 Phrases — In Reality TV Context

“That’s so extra.”
Overdramatic or over the top
“She cried for 20 minutes because she didn’t win the cooking challenge. That’s so extra.”
“Shade was thrown.”
Subtle indirect criticism
“She said ‘some people here clearly need the attention.’ Shade was definitely thrown.”
“It went viral.”
Spread rapidly online
“The moment went viral — it must have hit 50 million views before the episode even ended.”
“No cap.”
No lie, I’m serious
“That finale was the most stressful hour of television I’ve ever watched. No cap.”
These phrases appear naturally in discussions of media and entertainment. Note that “shade was thrown” uses passive voice — a B1.2 grammar point from this level. If learners notice, acknowledge it briefly. Require each learner to use at least one M5 phrase in their next speaking turn.
§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Modal Deduction — Applied to Reality TV

⚠ REAL SITUATION
You’re watching a reality show. A contestant suddenly starts crying about something that seems minor. A friendship seems forced. An elimination looks suspicious. You want to analyse what’s really going on — but you can’t know for certain. This is exactly when you need modal deduction.
✎ MODAL DEDUCTION — REVIEW + EXTENSION
You already know the basics. Today: applying them to analyse complex media situations.
SituationDeductionModal
I’m almost certain it’s trueHe must be acting — nobody cries that quickly.must + be/verb
I’m almost certain it’s falseThat can’t be real emotion — it’s too well-timed.can’t + be/verb
It’s possible but uncertainShe might be genuinely upset, or it could be strategy.might/could + be/verb
Past: almost certain it happenedThe producers must have planned that confrontation.must have + past participle
Past: almost certain it didn’tShe can’t have known the cameras were still rolling.can’t have + past participle

Carlos Mendez on Why We Watch

OPINION METER — Before you listen:
“Reality TV must be doing long-term psychological damage to both contestants and viewers.”

Carlos Mendez:

My clients sometimes ask me if I watch reality TV. My answer is yes. And here’s what I’ve learned from it as a coach: these shows must be studying human psychology more carefully than most business schools.

Think about what they do. They take a group of strangers, put them under pressure, create artificial competition, and then edit the footage to build a narrative. Viewers might be watching for entertainment, but what they’re actually consuming is a masterclass in group dynamics, leadership under stress, and the limits of human patience.

The manipulation is real. Producers can’t have designed these formats by accident. They know exactly how to create an audience investment in a contestant — and then how to destroy it. That’s not television. That’s behavioural engineering.

Now, is it harmful? It might be for some contestants. There is real research on anxiety and public exposure. But I think the more interesting question is what it tells us about viewers. Why do we vote, cheer, and feel genuine anger about people we’ve never met? That must say something important about our need for belonging and narrative — even if the narrative is manufactured.

💬 Quick: In 2 sentences, summarise what Carlos thinks reality TV reveals about human psychology. Try to use “must” or “might.”
Carlos models 4 modal deductions in the script — point them out after comprehension: “must be studying,” “might be watching,” “can’t have designed,” and “must say something.” Ask learners which sounds most certain and which sounds least certain. This reinforces the certainty spectrum (must > might > could). Revisit the Opinion Meter: “Has Carlos changed your position?”
§SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

Analyse the Show

RECALL
Carlos compares reality TV producers to behavioural engineers. What does he mean? Do you agree?
Use: “He must be suggesting that…” to start your paraphrase
DEDUCTION
Think of a specific reality TV moment you remember — real or described to you. What must have happened behind the scenes to create it?
Use must have / can’t have for past deductions about what producers did
PSYCHOLOGY
Why do we feel emotionally attached to contestants we’ve never met? What must be happening in our psychology when we watch?
Use “must be triggering…” / “it might be activating…” for deduction about internal states
DEBATE
Is watching reality TV a waste of time — or can it be genuinely educational? Build your argument using evidence.
Use: “That’s so extra” or “No cap” to signal your genuine opinion
WORK CONTEXT
Carlos says reality TV is “a masterclass in group dynamics and leadership under stress.” Could your company learn anything from how these shows work?
Think: team pressure, competition, elimination, vulnerability in meetings
CHALLENGE
Design a reality show set at a manufacturing plant in the Bajío. What would the competition be? Who would be eliminated first, and why must they be a strong character from TV’s perspective?
Use modal deduction to predict viewer reactions: “Viewers might connect with… The drama must come from…”
Q6 (design a reality show) is the highest-engagement question and always generates laughter and authentic production. Let it run. It forces learners to use all session vocabulary (contestants, drama, ratings, manipulation, eliminate) in a creative but grammatically demanding way. Q5 (work context) is the professional bridge — use it with more senior learners who need corporate relevance.
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“There must be something we’re not seeing.”
Use in meetings when a result or decision seems unexplained. Signals analytical thinking without direct accusation. Professional and measured.
“The ratings on this project are strong — we should understand why.”
Use “ratings” metaphorically to refer to any performance metric. Shows you track outcomes and want to learn from success, not just problems.
“That can’t have been the whole story.”
Use when reviewing a failed project or a surprising outcome. Invites deeper analysis without assigning blame. Works well with cross-functional teams.
