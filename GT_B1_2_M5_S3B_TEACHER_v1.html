⚡ Hot Take  ·  5 min

Fake News — Where Do You Stand?

“If you share something without verifying it, you must be held as responsible as the person who created it.”
Voted. Justify using must / can’t / might in 30 seconds. Your position must include a specific scenario.
This Hot Take is the most legally contentious of M5 — in some jurisdictions, knowingly sharing misinformation IS a legal offence. The “intent must matter” position is philosophically rich: can you be responsible without intent? Enforce modal deduction: anyone who argues without using must / can’t / might is asked to restate. This makes modal deduction a real-time accountability tool.
🗣 Pitch Round  ·  10 min

The Misinformation Tribunal

A viral story caused real financial harm. Each pair represents one party. 2 min prep — 2 min pitch of your defence or prosecution argument.

PARTY A — THE PROSECUTION
A social media account spread false information that a publicly traded company was going bankrupt. The stock dropped 18%. You’re prosecuting the account owner. They must have known the story was false — it was obviously fabricated and the damage was foreseeable.
Must use: misinformation, credibility, source, spread, modal deduction
PARTY B — THE DEFENCE
Your client shared an article that seemed credible. The headline was professionally written. The source looked legitimate. They can’t have known it was fabricated without specialised fact-checking tools. They might have acted carelessly, but not maliciously.
Must use: verify, bias, credibility, context, modal deduction
PARTY C — THE PLATFORM
Your platform removed the post within 4 hours of it going viral. You have 500 million users. You can’t review every piece of content in real time. Your algorithm flagged it, your team acted. The platform must not bear legal responsibility for individual users’ actions.
Must use: algorithm, regulation, content, filter, modal deduction
PARTY D — THE JOURNALIST
You wrote a factual article that was misquoted and taken out of context to create the false story. You must be exonerated completely. The misinformation must have been a deliberate distortion of your work. Your credibility and career have been damaged by the confusion.
Must use: source, headline, propaganda, fact-check, modal deduction
PREP TIME
PITCH TIME
The Tribunal format is excellent for modal deduction because legal language REQUIRES deduction: “the defendant must have known,” “the platform can’t have been unaware,” “the harm might have been foreseeable.” After pitches, the group votes on verdict — not by sympathy, but by strength of modal deduction argument. Who used the strongest evidence-based reasoning? This rewards grammar use naturally.
🏃 Relay  ·  8 min

Source Chain Relay

Each learner is one link in the chain. The original fact is at the top. Watch how it mutates. Each person explains what must have changed at their link — and why.

📰
LINK 1 — THE ORIGINAL STUDY
“A university study of 200 participants found that people who drink 3+ coffees per day had 12% lower rates of a specific liver condition.”
Your task: Read this accurately. What must a careful reader notice about the limitations?
📊
LINK 2 — THE HEALTH BLOG
The blogger summarises the study but removes “specific liver condition” and writes: “Coffee drinkers are 12% healthier than non-coffee drinkers.”
Your task: What must have been lost in translation? What can’t the blogger claim?
🎙
LINK 3 — THE VIRAL TWEET
A social media account posts: “BREAKING: Scientists PROVE coffee makes you healthier. Big Pharma doesn’t want you to know.”
Your task: What must be wrong with this? What might the conspiracy framing be designed to do?
💬
LINK 4 — THE WHATSAPP FORWARD
Your aunt forwards it in the family group chat with: “My doctor told me to stop drinking coffee. They must be lying to us. Share this with everyone you care about.”
Your task: At this point, the original fact is unrecognisable. What must have driven each distortion? What can’t be recovered now?
Group close: “At which link in this chain could the misinformation have been stopped? What must have changed at that point to prevent the final version?” Use modal deduction for the counterfactual: “If the blogger had…, the tweet might never have…”
The Source Chain is S3B’s unique format — distinct from S1B (Platform cards) and S2B (Editing Room). It maps the mutation of a real piece of information through 4 recognisable vectors: academic study → health blog → viral social media → WhatsApp. The WhatsApp link is deliberately the final one because in Mexico, it’s where misinformation reaches the widest audience. Link 4 is the most emotionally resonant — “your aunt forwards it” is personal. Assign it to the learner who will engage with it most honestly.
⚡ Speed Round  ·  6 min

30 Seconds — Fake News Edition

Give 3 specific signals that a headline must be clickbait or misinformation. Use “must” for each signal.
ALL CAPS, no date, emotional language, no named source…
Your manager just shared an article in the team chat that you think might be false. How do you respond professionally in English?
Use: “That can’t be the whole story — let me verify before we act on it.”
What must an algorithm be designed to do that makes it structurally hostile to accurate news? Explain in 30 seconds.
Engagement > accuracy. Emotion > truth. Algorithm must maximise time-on-platform.
Apply Carlos’s three questions to a piece of information you received this week. Say them out loud.
Whose interest? What am I being sold? What am I comparing myself to?
In what professional context might misinformation directly harm your company? Name a specific scenario and its consequences.
Supplier bankruptcy rumour, safety incident misreporting, regulatory misinformation…
Design a 30-second fact-checking protocol for your team that must be followed before sharing any external information. 3 steps.
Use must / can’t for each step. Make it realistic for a Mexican manufacturing context.
30s SPEED TIMER
Card 4 (Carlos’s 3 questions) tests whether learners have truly internalised the S3A framework. Card 5 (professional context) produces the most contextually relevant language for Bajío manufacturing learners — supplier rumour and regulatory misinformation are real daily risks. Card 6 (fact-check protocol) is a direct skills transfer: the language produced here is usable in a real team protocol document. If time permits, compile the group’s 3-step protocols into one shared list on the board.
🌟 Solo Challenge  ·  8 min

The Press Correction

Your Scenario
A false story has been circulating that your company has been fined by environmental regulators for illegal waste disposal. It’s completely untrue. A local news site published it without verifying the source. You are the company’s communications director. You have 90 seconds to issue a verbal correction on live radio.

Be clear, credible, and professional. Use modal deduction to address what must have happened and what can’t be true.
✅ Use must have / can’t have for what happened
✅ Use might be for what you’re still investigating
✅ Use at least 4 S3A vocabulary words
✅ Demand a correction from the outlet — professionally, not aggressively
❌ No notes. Radio is live. No second take.
90s SOLO TIMER
The Press Correction is one of the most high-stakes professional communication scenarios in English. Modal deduction is not decoration here — it’s the mechanism of accountability.
💰 After the Solo Challenge: “You just gave a live radio correction in English. That’s crisis communications. At B2.1, you’ll write the formal written correction, the internal incident report, and the legal letter to the outlet. The English you need for that is a direct extension of what you’re doing right now.”

🎯 3 Phrases From This Session — Use Them This Week:
“That story can’t have been verified.”
Use it when questioning a claim in a meeting or report. Professional, analytical, non-accusatory. Forces the room to produce a source.
“We must address this before it spreads further.”
Use in any crisis or issue management context. “Must” creates urgency without panic. Works in communications, compliance, and operations meetings.
“The source might be credible, but the headline can’t be.”
Use to distinguish between a legitimate source and a misleading presentation of data. Shows analytical depth. Works in any review of external information.
