§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

Quiet Quitting — Lazy or Self-Care?

Q1  Personal
Have you ever done exactly what your job description says — nothing more, nothing less? How did it feel?
Q2  Work
At your company, is there pressure to work extra hours or do more than your role requires? Where does that pressure come from?
Q3  Opinion
If an employee does their job perfectly but refuses to do anything extra, should their employer be worried? Why or why not?
Q4  Challenge
Describe the concept of quiet quitting without using the words quiet or quitting. You have 30 seconds.
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Complete this sentence out loud: “If I only did exactly what my job description says, I would …” — It doesn’t need to be perfect. Just say it.
Q4 is the GT twist: forcing learners to describe the concept with different vocabulary activates productive vocabulary search. Give them 30 real seconds, then share as a group. It doubles as a preview of session vocabulary. For Q1, normalise the experience — many learners will have done this without a name for it.
This topic lands differently depending on seniority. Engineers and coordinators may relate strongly; managers may feel defensive. Acknowledge both sides early: “Today we’re not judging — we’re understanding.” Creates psychological safety for honest discussion.
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“If companies truly valued their employees, would quiet quitting even exist?”

Quiet quitting became a global conversation in 2022. Millions of workers — especially in manufacturing, logistics, and tech — decided to stop going above and beyond at work. Some call it a healthy boundary. Others call it a slow resignation. What is it really? And what does it tell us about the relationship between employees and companies?

Give learners 90 seconds to discuss in pairs before opening to the group. The question is designed to provoke — let them push back on it. If they agree too quickly, ask: “But what if the employee is just lazy?” to create productive tension and more language use.

§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Second Conditional — Your Tool for Hypotheticals

⚠ REAL SITUATION
Your colleague says: “I work 60 hours a week but my salary hasn’t changed in 3 years. What would you do in my situation?”

You want to answer — but how do you talk about a hypothetical situation that isn’t real for you right now? You need the Second Conditional.
✎ THE TOOL — SECOND CONDITIONAL
If + past simple  →  would + base verb
Use it for: imaginary present/future situations • advice • hypothetical opinions at work
FormExample
AffirmativeIf I had more support, I would stay at this company.
NegativeIf they didn’t ignore feedback, employees wouldn’t disengage.
QuestionWhat would you do if you earned 30% less?
Were (formal/all subjects)If I were the manager, I would change the bonus structure.
⚠ Common error: “If I would have…” → WRONG. The IF clause never uses ‘would.’
ACTIVITY A — Choose the correct form
1. If the company _____ employees for overtime, quiet quitting _____ so common.
A. compensated / wouldn’t be
B. would compensate / isn’t
C. compensates / won’t be
2. What _____ you _____ if your manager asked you to work every Saturday without extra pay?
A. would / do
B. will / did
C. do / would
3. If I _____ the operations coordinator at Solaris, I _____ a meeting about workload expectations.
A. am / will schedule
B. were / would schedule
C. was / will schedule
4. Employees _____ more if companies _____ better recognition programs.
A. would engage / have
B. would engage / had
C. engage / would have
💬 Quick: Tell your partner: “If my company offered me _____, I would _____.” Use something real from your work life.

Carlos Mendez:

I’ve been coaching executives in the Bajío region for twelve years. And the question I get most often now is: “Carlos, why are my best people doing just enough to keep their jobs?” My answer is always the same: because you taught them to.

Here’s the reality. If a company only rewarded seniority and not performance, employees would stop performing above expectations. If managers never acknowledged effort, people would eventually stop making it. This isn’t a generation problem. It’s a systems problem.

Now, I understand the other side too. I speak to employees who say: “If I worked 70 hours a week, would my salary change? No. So why would I do it?” That logic is not lazy. That logic is rational.

If I were designing a corporate culture today, I would build recognition into the system — not as an extra, but as a core element. Because at the end of the day, loyalty doesn’t come from a contract. It comes from feeling valued.

The question for every company isn’t “how do we stop quiet quitting?” It’s “what would it take for employees to want to do more?” That’s a much more interesting question. And it’s one most companies are afraid to answer honestly.

§SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

Your Turn to Argue

RECALL
In Carlos’s coaching experience, what do executives usually ask him about employee motivation?
Grammar hint: Use simple present or past — “He says…” / “He mentioned…”
OPINION
Do you agree that quiet quitting is a “systems problem” rather than a personal attitude problem? Why?
Grammar hint: Use Second Conditional — “If companies changed X, employees would…”
WORK CONTEXT
At your company, what incentives or recognition systems exist for going above and beyond? Are they effective?
Grammar hint: Try “If there were more…, I would…”
HYPOTHETICAL
If you were the HR director at your company, what ONE change would you make to reduce disengagement?
Grammar hint: “If I were HR director, I would…” — note: ‘were’ for all subjects in formal conditionals
DEBATE
Is it a red flag when a company talks about “work family” culture? Or is that a positive sign? Justify your answer.
Grammar hint: Use the M4 colloquial phrase — “At the end of the day, I think…”
CHALLENGE
Someone says: “If you quiet quit, you’re just lazy.” How would you respond? Build a counterargument using session vocabulary.
Grammar hint: Use “I understand your point, but if you consider…” to concede before countering
Questions 4 and 5 are designed to produce Second Conditional naturally. Question 6 is the production peak: learners need to integrate session vocabulary into a structured counterargument. Model the structure once: “I understand your point, but if companies valued loyalty, employees wouldn’t need to protect themselves.” Then let pairs run it.
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“If I were in that position, I would…”
Use it when a colleague shares a problem and you want to give advice or show empathy without being prescriptive.
“At the end of the day, it’s about…”
Use it to close a discussion, pivot to the key point, or summarise your position in meetings or 1-on-1s.
“That’s a red flag for me.”
Use it when evaluating a proposal, process, or situation that concerns you. Professional but direct — perfect for English-language meetings.
