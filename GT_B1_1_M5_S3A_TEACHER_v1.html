💪 Warm-Up — Exercise & Health Routines

5–8 min
Module 5 · Session 3A — Exercise & Health Routines · Why is it so hard to stay consistent?
🔁 Module 5 so far: S1A — Reported statements ("She said she was tired.") · S2A — Reported questions ("She asked me what I did to recharge.") · Today: we apply both to talk about health habits — reporting what people say, ask, and advise about exercise.
Q1 — Honest check
Do you exercise regularly? If yes, what do you do? If no — be honest — what gets in the way?
Q2 — The wall
Have you ever started an exercise routine and then stopped? What happened? What was the exact moment you quit?
Q3 — What works
What has actually worked for you to stay consistent — not just for a week, but long-term? Is there a trick, a mindset, a person who helped?
Q4 — Work angle
Does your company offer anything to support physical health — a gym, flexible hours, a wellness program? Do you use it?
⚡ Quick Win — 20 seconds
Say this sentence out loud: "My doctor told me I needed to exercise more consistently, but he also asked me what had stopped me before."
Notice it has both a reported statement AND a reported question in one sentence. That is today's target level.
Teacher note — Warm-Up: Q2 is the session's emotional anchor. "The exact moment you quit" is specific and memorable — it forces students to recall a concrete story rather than a vague feeling. Stories generate vocabulary naturally. Listen for: "I was too tired," "I didn't have time," "I got injured" — these become your S5 grammar material.

Quick Win tip: After the timer, ask: "Can you identify the reported statement and the reported question in that sentence?" This is a 30-second grammar review that primes §5 without feeling like a drill.
Pedagogical layer: Exercise habits are a universal struggle for shift workers and corporate employees in the Bajio. The 6am–6pm factory shift cycle leaves almost no time. If students say "I don't exercise because of my schedule," do not move on — that is the real conversation. It validates the topic and generates authentic language.

🗣️ Speaking Topic — Exercise & Health Routines

8 min

Why is it hard to maintain healthy habits? What has actually worked for you?

Move beyond "I don't have time." Dig into the psychology — motivation, discipline, failure, restart. This is a topic where everyone has a real story.

Exercise & Health Routines · 3 Discussion Rounds
T→S Round 1
Tell me: what is the biggest obstacle to maintaining a health routine in your life right now? Is it time, motivation, discipline, or something else?
S↔S Round 2
Ask your partner: "Has anyone ever given you advice about exercise or healthy habits? What did they say? Did you follow it?" Report their answer: "My partner said that someone had told them..."
Class Round 3
Class vote: "Motivation is not enough — you need discipline and consistency to build any real health habit." Agree? Who has evidence from their own experience?

Exercise habits — natural B1.1 response:

"Honestly, the biggest obstacle for me is consistency. I start well, but after two weeks I lose my motivation. A friend of mine told me that he had found a solution: he stopped tracking results and just focused on showing up. He asked me why I always quit after a good start — and I didn't have an answer. It's hard to stay consistent when you work long shifts. But I'm trying to build the habit now — even ten minutes counts."

Note: S3 vocabulary · Reported statement + reported question combined · S3 colloquial phrase

Teacher note — Round 2: The double-layer reporting in Round 2 (“my partner said that someone had told them”) is ambitious for B1.1 — but achievable and impressive when it works. Do not force it. If students produce it naturally, celebrate it explicitly. If they default to simpler forms, accept and upgrade: "Good. Now can you add: and she also told me that..."

💬 Colloquial English — Exercise & Habit Phrases

5 min
3 phrases about exercise and habits that native speakers actually use
"I feel better when I exercise."
When to use
When explaining why you exercise, even when you don't want to. "I know it's hard to start, but I always feel better when I exercise." Simple, true, and immediately relatable.
"It's hard to stay consistent."
When to use
When being honest about your health routine. "I want to exercise more — it's just hard to stay consistent when my schedule changes every week." Shows self-awareness without excuses.
"I'm trying to build the habit."
When to use
When you are in the process of forming a new routine. "I started going to the gym twice a week — I'm trying to build the habit before I add more days." Sounds committed and realistic.
"Once you get into it, it gets easier."
When to use
When encouraging someone who is struggling to start a habit. "The first two weeks are the hardest. Once you get into it, it gets easier — I promise." A classic motivator in English.
Teacher note — Colloquial: "Once you get into it" is a conditional structure (“once” = when this happens). At B1.1 this is pre-exposure to first conditional logic without naming it. Ask: "Can you use this phrase with something at work? Once you get into the report, it gets easier." Students who extend the pattern are working at the upper range of B1.1.

Drill: "Turn to your partner and say: 'I'm trying to build the habit of...'" — they complete it with something real. Three students share. This is a 60-second oral drill that embeds the phrase without making it feel like drilling.
Upsell layer: "It's hard to stay consistent" is also the phrase for work contexts — "It's hard to stay consistent with client follow-ups when you're managing 10 accounts." After the drill, ask: "Can this phrase apply to anything at work?" This connects personal language to professional contexts and signals curriculum depth.

⚡ Grammar — Reported Speech: Combining Statements & Questions

12–15 min
Grammar · Combining reported statements and questions in one narrative
🎯 Real Work Situation

You had a conversation with your occupational health nurse at work. She gave you advice about exercise and asked about your current habits. Now your manager asks: "How did that meeting go?"

You need to report both what she said (statement) and what she asked you (question) in the same natural narrative — without switching back to direct speech halfway through.

🔧 The Tool — Full Reported Speech Narrative

Real conversations mix statements and questions. In Reported Speech, you handle them in sequence using the same rules: said/told + statement word order and asked + question word order (no inversion). The key is smooth connectors: "She said... She also told me... Then she asked me..."

Direct: "You need to exercise more. Do you have a routine?"
Reported: "She told me I needed to exercise more. Then she asked me if I had a routine."

Direct: "Small steps are better than big changes. What has stopped you before?"
Reported: "She said small steps were better. She also asked what had stopped me before."
Direct speechReported formType
"You need to commit."She told me I needed to commit.Statement — told + person
"Small steps work better."She said small steps worked better.Statement — said (no person)
"Have you tracked your progress?"She asked if I had tracked my progress.Yes/No question — if + subject + verb
"What stops you?"She asked what stopped me.Wh- question — no inversion
"Can you commit to 30 days?"She asked if I could commit to 30 days.Yes/No — can → could
Common errors in narratives:
✗ "She said me I needed to exercise." → She told me I needed to exercise.
✗ "She asked what does stop me." → She asked what stopped me. (no inversion, tense shift)
✗ Mixing tenses: "She told me I needed to exercise and she asks if I had a routine." → Keep the past reporting verbs consistent.
🗣️ Micro-Speaking: Report a real health conversation you have had — with a doctor, a friend, or a colleague. Use at least one statement ("She said...") and one question ("She also asked me..."). 45 seconds. Go.
Teacher note — Grammar S3A: The key pedagogical step here is consistency of tense across the whole narrative. Students who master individual reported sentences often lose control when they put multiple sentences together. Write on the board: "All reporting verbs in the same time zone: said / told / asked." Draw a timeline if helpful. Then use one student's §2 story as the class practice text — transform it together.

🎧 Listening — The Gym Conversation

10–12 min
Listening · Rodrigo reports a conversation with a colleague about fitness · Vertex Logistics, Celaya

Rodrigo Vega at Vertex Logistics tells a colleague about a conversation he had with a fitness coach at the company's new wellness program. Listen for a mix of reported statements AND reported questions — and notice how he connects them naturally.

The Wellness Program — Vertex Logistics, Celaya
B1.1 · M5 · S3A · Voice: Adam (American English) · Speed: 0.85 · ElevenLabs
So the company launched a wellness program last month, and I signed up. They assigned each of us a fitness coach for an initial consultation. I want to tell you about my session because I found it really useful. The coach introduced herself and said the goal of the program was not to make us athletes, but to help us build small, sustainable habits. She said that most people failed not because they lacked motivation, but because they tried to do too much too fast. She told me that consistency was the key, and that even ten minutes of movement a day was better than nothing. Then she started asking me questions. She asked me what my current routine looked like. I told her I had no real routine. She asked me what had stopped me from exercising in the past. I said it was the schedule — the early shifts made it difficult. She said she understood and told me that many of the plant workers had said the same thing. She asked me if I had ever tried exercising right after work, before going home. I said I had not thought of that. She told me that several colleagues had tried it and found it worked well because they did not have to restart their motivation after dinner. Finally, she asked me to commit to just three sessions in the first week — nothing more. She said the goal was to build the habit, not to track results yet. She told me to focus on showing up, not on performance. I have to say, it was the most practical advice about exercise I have ever received.
Listening note: This script is the richest in the module for Reported Speech density. Before the second listen, assign different students to listen for: (A) reported statements with "said/told," (B) reported questions with "asked." After listening, compile their findings on the board — it becomes a class grammar map of the audio. Students feel the grammar functioning in a real narrative.
📊 Comprehension — Multiple Choice
1. What did the coach say was the goal of the wellness program?
2. What did Rodrigo say had stopped him from exercising in the past?
3. What did the coach ask Rodrigo to commit to in the first week?
✅ True / False
The coach told Rodrigo that most people failed because they tried to do too much too fast.
Rodrigo said he already had a consistent exercise routine before the program.
The coach told Rodrigo to focus on showing up rather than on performance.

T→S Question 1 — Doctor's Advice
Has a doctor, nurse, or health professional ever given you advice about exercise or health habits? Report what they said AND what they asked you. Use at least 3 sentences of Reported Speech.
Grammar hint: "She told me that... She also said... Then she asked me if..."
S↔S Question 2 — The Best Advice
Ask your partner: "What is the best advice anyone has ever given you about staying healthy — at work or in life?" Then report their answer to the class, including at least one thing they said AND one thing the original advisor asked.
Grammar hint: "My partner said that someone had told them... The person had also asked them..."
S↔S Question 3 — The Failing Routine
Describe a time you started a health routine but stopped. Tell your partner: what you were trying to do, what happened, and what someone said or asked you about it at the time. Use Reported Speech for what was said.
Vocabulary hint: "stick to," "consistency," "restart," "fail," "I tried to build the habit but..."
Class Question 4 — Company Responsibility
Should companies like Vertex Logistics provide time and resources for employee health? Report what Rodrigo's coach said about the program — do you agree with her approach? Give 2 reasons.
Grammar hint: "The coach said that... She also told them... I agree/disagree because..."
Class Question 5 — Your Coach Moment
You are a fitness coach. Give your partner one piece of advice about health habits (statement) and ask them one question about their barriers (question). Then switch. Report the conversation: "As the coach, I told my partner... I also asked them..."
Grammar hint: This produces Reported Speech from the coach's perspective — a creative reversal
⚡ Solo Challenge — 90 seconds

Report a real health-related conversation you have had — with anyone. Tell us who it was, what they said (at least 2 statements), and what they asked you (at least 1 question). Connect naturally: "She said... She also told me... Then she asked me..."

Teacher note — Q5 Coach Role-Play: This is the session's most creative activity. Students generating Reported Speech about their own coaching statements moves them from passive reporters to active architects of the structure. It is also excellent preparation for the S3B role-play. If time is tight, do Q5 only — it contains Q1–Q4 embedded within the role reversal.
Speaking layers: Layer 1 — Are they using Reported Speech at all or defaulting to direct? Layer 2 — Are they combining statements and questions in one narrative, or only using one type? Layer 3 — Are they using natural connectors ("she also told me," "then she asked")? The connector layer is the B1.1 fluency marker. Target it explicitly in feedback.
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work
3 Phrases for This Week
PHRASE 1
"She told me consistency was more important than intensity."
A complete Reported Speech sentence using two key vocabulary words — and a genuinely useful idea. Use it in any conversation about health, habits, or even work performance. You can adapt it: "My manager told me consistency was more important than effort in short bursts."
PHRASE 2
"I'm trying to build the habit."
Your honest, professional answer when anyone asks about your exercise, learning, or any new routine. It signals effort without overpromising. Use it in English conversations about anything you are working on: "I'm trying to build the habit of speaking English at lunch."
PHRASE 3
"It's hard to stay consistent, but I'm working on it."
The most authentic thing you can say about any ongoing challenge. In English, "I'm working on it" shows agency and effort without defensiveness. Use it about exercise, English practice, time management — it works for everything.
