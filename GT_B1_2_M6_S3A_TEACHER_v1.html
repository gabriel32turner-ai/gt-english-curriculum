§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

How Do You Handle Failure?

★ GT ORIGINAL — Teacher Models First
Before asking students to share, share your own brief example: a professional failure, what happened, what you learned, and what changed. Keep it to 60 seconds. This models vulnerability and makes it safe for learners to be honest. Without this, most corporate learners will give diplomatic non-answers.
Q1  Honest
Think of a professional failure — not a small mistake, a real setback. Although you might not want to share the details, what was the first thing you felt? And what was the first thing you did?
Q2  Opinion
Is failure talked about openly at your company? Despite the research that says psychological safety improves performance, most organisations still punish failure. Why does that gap exist?
Q3  Debate
Is there such a thing as a “good” failure — one you would repeat, knowing what you know now? As a result of which failure did you grow the most?
Q4  Work
Think of someone you admire at work. Although they appear very confident and successful today, what kind of failure do you think they’ve experienced? How do you know?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Complete this sentence out loud: “Despite [something that went wrong], I managed to [what you did]. As a result, I [what changed].” — A three-part failure story with two linking words in under 30 seconds.
GT Original: this is the most emotionally significant session in B1.2. Corporate learners rarely have space to talk honestly about failure in English — or in any language at work. The teacher modelling vulnerability first is non-negotiable. Without it, most learners will give safe, polished answers. Q2 (failure culture at company) is the most professionally relevant — let it run if energy is high. Q3 (good failure) connects to the growth mindset vocabulary in §3.
The emotional safety of this session is the product. If learners feel judged, they won’t speak authentically — and the language produced will be flat and generic. Frame from the start: “Today we’re talking about something most English classes never touch. Real failure. Real stories. This is a safe room.”
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“Although failure is universally considered painful, the most successful professionals seem to treat it as a tool. What separates the ones who recover from those who don’t?”

Research in psychology consistently shows that resilience — the ability to recover from setbacks — is more predictive of long-term success than talent, intelligence, or experience alone. Despite this, most professional environments are structured to avoid failure rather than learn from it. As a result, many talented people develop what psychologists call “failure avoidance” — they stop taking risks that could accelerate their growth because the professional cost of failing is too high. The question is not whether you fail. It is how quickly and honestly you process it.

Point out the linking words used in the speaking topic text: “Although,” “Despite this,” “As a result.” Ask learners after 90 seconds: “What’s the difference between ‘despite’ and ‘although’ in terms of what follows?” Let them figure out the rule: although + subject + verb; despite + noun/-ing. This reinforces S1A grammar naturally.

Module 6 Phrases — In Failure & Resilience Context

“No looking back.”
Committed to moving forward; no regret
“Despite how hard that failure was, I processed it and moved on. No looking back.”
“It’s a game-changer.”
Something that completely transforms a situation
“That failure was a game-changer — although it hurt, as a result it changed everything about how I approach risk.”
“I’ve come a long way.”
Reflecting on how much you’ve grown
“Whereas before I would avoid all risk, now I take calculated ones. I’ve come a long way since that setback.”
“I’m figuring it out.”
In the process of finding a way forward
“After that project fell apart, I didn’t have all the answers. I was figuring it out — and that was OK.”
By S3A, all 5 M6 phrases should be familiar. Test retention without showing the screen: “What are the 5 M6 phrases?” Anyone who can produce all 5 with their meaning has genuinely internalised them. In this session, “I’m figuring it out” is particularly resonant because failure often means being mid-process — not having the answer yet is a valid and honest English response to professional difficulty.
§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Linking Words — Applied to Personal Narrative

⚠ REAL SITUATION
You’re in a job interview. The interviewer says: “Tell me about a time you failed and what you learned from it.” This is one of the most common interview questions at B1+ English level. A generic answer sounds weak. A structured answer using linking words sounds professional, self-aware, and credible.
✎ LINKING WORDS IN PERSONAL NARRATIVE — THE STORY STRUCTURE
Failure stories in English need structure. These linking words provide it: they connect context, contrast, consequence, and conclusion.
Story phaseLinking wordExample
Setting the sceneAlthough / Even thoughAlthough I had prepared well, the project went completely wrong.
Admitting the difficultyDespite + -ingDespite working harder than ever, the outcome was a failure.
Explaining the consequenceAs a result / ConsequentlyAs a result, I had to completely rethink my approach.
The lesson or growthHowever / NeverthelessHowever, this taught me something I couldn’t have learned any other way.
The forward moveFurthermore / In additionFurthermore, it changed how my whole team approaches risk.
🌟 The structure: Although [context] → Despite [difficulty] → As a result [consequence] → However [lesson] → Furthermore [growth]. Five linking words. One complete, professional failure story.

Carlos Mendez on Failure & the Growth Mindset

OPINION METER — Before you listen:
“Although failure is painful, those who succeed most are generally those who have failed most — and reflected most honestly on those failures.”

Carlos Mendez:

In every coaching session I run, I ask the same question: “Tell me about your most significant failure.” Although most people are initially reluctant, the answer to this question tells me more about a person’s professional character than any CV or performance review ever could.

What I look for is not what happened. What happened is in the past and cannot be changed. What I look for is the relationship the person has with the story. Whereas some people tell the failure story as if it’s still happening to them — present tense, emotionally activated — others tell it as a chapter that ended and led somewhere.

Despite the pain of failure, the most resilient professionals I know are those who have learned to distinguish between what they did and who they are. A failed project is not a failed person. A setback is not a verdict. As a result of making that distinction, they recover faster, reflect better, and try again with more intelligence than before.

Furthermore, I would argue that the ability to talk about failure clearly and without shame is itself a professional skill — one that distinguishes leaders from high-performers who peaked early and got comfortable.

PROFESSIONAL
Your company just lost a major client. You were part of the team. In a meeting, you’re asked to reflect on what happened. How do you speak about it professionally — honestly, without blame, with learning?
Use: although / despite / as a result / however — one per phase of your reflection
INTERVIEW
Answer this question as if you’re in an English-language job interview: “Tell me about a time you failed and what you learned from it.” Use the 5-part structure. 90 seconds max.
This is a real interview question. Structure beats content every time at B1.2 level.
DEBATE
Is it possible for a company to have a genuine “growth mindset” if it systematically punishes individuals who fail? Build an argument using evidence and linking words.
Use: although, whereas, as a result, furthermore — each for a different part of your argument
CHALLENGE
Describe the most useful failure you’ve ever seen or experienced. Not your worst — your most useful. What made it useful? Use all 5 M6 phrases in your answer if possible.
although, despite, as a result + game-changer, figuring it out, I’ve come a long way
Q4 (interview question) is the most practically valuable speaking task in M6. Every learner at B1.2 may eventually interview in English — or at least present in English to international stakeholders. The ability to answer “tell me about a failure” with structure and honesty is a genuine differentiator. If time allows, do Q4 as a group: one learner answers, the group evaluates structure (not content) using the five linking words as the checklist.
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“Despite the setback, we’re moving forward.”
Use in any project review or team meeting after something has gone wrong. Acknowledges the difficulty without dwelling on it. Signals leadership and forward momentum.
“As a result of that experience, we changed our approach.”
Use in retrospective meetings, client presentations, and audit reviews. Transforms failure into process improvement. One of the most professional phrases in this module.
“Although it didn’t work this time, the lesson is clear.”
Use to close any discussion about something that failed. The structure acknowledges failure, pivots to learning, and ends with confidence. Works in any professional context.
