§ 01
Warm-Up — Date Challenge
5–8 min
🎬 Scenario — Your Inbox, Monday Morning
David Park (Phoenix) just sent you an email with several key Q2 dates. The problem: he wrote them in American format — numbers only. You need to say them out loud in full English on the 9 AM call.
"Hi team — quick reminders: Q2 kickoff 4/7, proposal deadline 4/21, mid-review 5/15, final delivery 6/30. See you at 9!"
👉 Can you read those dates in English? "4/7 is..." — try. It doesn't have to be perfect yet.
Expected A1.2 output: Student should attempt "April seventh" or "the seventh of April." If they say only "four seven" — that's the perfect starting point for the session. Don't correct yet.
⚡ Quick Win — 20 seconds
Say your next work event in English: "I have a meeting on Tuesday at 10. The next deadline is on March 20th."
Activation questions (after the first attempt)
"What's the difference between 4 (four) and 4th (fourth)? When do you use each one?"
"If the meeting is at your office and David is coming from the airport — how do you ask him how long it takes to get there?"
"What's your next real work deadline? Say the full date in English."
Layer 1 · Pedagogical
  • ACTION: Show David's email on screen and have the student read it aloud. The moment they stumble on "4/7" is the hook for the whole session.
  • Connect with S1A: "In S1A you learned 'on March 20th.' Today you'll be able to say any date of the year — not just one example."
  • The US numeric format (4/7 = April 7th, NOT July 4th in US context) is a real practical point. Many students at companies with international contact face this exact problem.
  • Timing: 5 min max. The core is §4 (grammar) and §5 (reading). Arrive with high energy.
Layer 2 · Engagement
  • "You receive David's kind of email at your company. This session is exactly so you never again have to guess how to say a date in English."
Layer 3 · Retention & Upsell
  • "At the end of M1 you present your full agenda — dates, deadlines, frequency. This session is the missing piece to do that with full confidence."

§ 04
Grammar — How long does it take? / How far is it?
12–15 min
🔴 The Problem

David Park flies to Celaya for a meeting and asks: "How long does it take from the airport to your office?" — You know the answer (20 minutes by taxi) but you don't know how to answer the question correctly in English. You also don't know how to ask about distance.

↓
🟣 The Tool — How long / How far

Two essential questions for any context of corporate travel, hosting clients, or coordinating logistics.

Question Structure Standard answer
How long does it take? How long + does it take + (to get there)? It takes + [time] + by [transport].
It takes 25 minutes by taxi.
How long does it take to + verb? How long + does it take + to get to [place]? It takes about an hour to get there.
To get to the office from downtown.
How far is it? How far + is it + (from [A] to [B])? It's about [distance] from [place].
It's about 15 km from the airport.
How far is it to [place]? How far + is it + to [destination]? It's not far — about 10 minutes on foot.
Walking distance from the hotel.
Key rule: "How long" asks about time → answer with "It takes [time]." "How far" asks about distance → answer with "It's [distance/time]." Both questions use "does it" or "is it" — never mix them up.
How long → It takes…
It takes 20 minutes by taxi.
It takes about an hour by bus.
It takes 5 minutes on foot.
It takes 2 hours by plane.
How far → It's…
It's 15 km from the airport.
It's not far — about 10 minutes.
It's walking distance from here.
It's about 3 hours by car.
⚠️ Common Errors — Celaya Edition
✗ "How long is it to get there?"→✓ "How long does it take to get there?"
"How long is it" asks about distance, not time. For time: "How long does it take?"
✗ "It takes 20 minutes in taxi."→✓ "It takes 20 minutes by taxi."
Transport always uses "by" — by taxi / by car / by bus / by plane. Exception: "on foot."
✗ "How far does it take?"→✓ "How far is it?" / "How long does it take?"
Don't mix them. "Far" + "is it" · "long" + "does it take."
✗ "The meeting is in April 21st."→✓ "The meeting is on April 21st."
Specific dates always use "on." Only use "in" for months or years without an exact date.
