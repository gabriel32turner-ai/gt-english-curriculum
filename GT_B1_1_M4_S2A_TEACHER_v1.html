§1 Warm-Up — Your Screen Time Check Activate digital life vocabulary · Connect to real phone habits ⏱ 5 MIN
📱 How many hours a day do you spend on your phone? Do you know your weekly screen time?
→ T→S: Ask everyone. Compare numbers. Who has the highest? Note answers on the board.
💼 What do you use your phone for most — work, social media, or entertainment?
→ T→S: Push for specifics — WhatsApp for work? Instagram after dinner? YouTube before sleep?
🔄 Have you ever tried to reduce your screen time? What happened?
→ S→S: Pair up. Push for story — "I tried for 3 days and then…"
😰 If your phone was taken away for 24 hours, what would you feel?
→ S→S: Answers reveal dependency level. Connect to today's passive grammar: "I would feel disconnected…"
⚡
Pedagogical Layering
Layer 1 (passive naturally emerging): "My phone is used for…" · Layer 2 (present perfect): "Have you ever tracked your screen time?" · Layer 3 (critical): "What does your screen time say about your priorities?"
⏱ 90-Second Warm-Up Timer — Start when first question is asked
§2 Speaking — Is Your Screen Time a Problem? 3 rounds · Passive voice emerging naturally · Model B1.1 answer ⏱ 8 MIN
Session Topic
Screen Time — How Much Is Too Much?
Round 1 — 60s · T→S
Describe your typical day on your phone — morning to night. When do you pick it up first? When do you put it down last?
"My phone is picked up before I even get out of bed — the alarm is set on it, so it's right there. Then messages are checked, Instagram is opened... by 9am I've already spent 40 minutes on it. At night it's the same thing in reverse."
Round 2 — 90s · S→S
Your partner: When is screen time a problem? When is it OK? Are there situations where you should disconnect completely?
"I think it becomes a problem when it's used automatically — when you pick it up without thinking why. For work, fine. For staying in touch, fine. But when dinner is being eaten while Instagram is being scrolled — that's a problem."
Round 3 — 90s · Class
Should companies be required by law to show users their screen time data? Should apps be designed with usage limits built in?
"I think data should be shown clearly — not buried. But limits shouldn't be imposed by law. That said, these apps are designed to maximize time spent — not to help you. So maybe some regulation is needed. The question is: who decides what's too much?"
Model Answer — Color-Coded
"My phone is used for about 5 hours a day — mostly for work, but also Instagram and YouTube. Notifications are sent every few minutes and they're designed to bring you back. I tried to reduce my usage last month — I set limits, but they were bypassed after 2 days. What I've realized is that the apps are built to be checked constantly. It's not really about willpower."
■ Passive Voice ■ Target Vocabulary ■ Colloquial Phrase
📘 Context Note Notice how the model answer uses passive voice naturally: "notifications are sent", "apps are built", "limits were bypassed." This is intentional — tech language is full of passive voice because it de-centers the agent (the company). Help students see that passive in tech is often a rhetorical choice, not just grammar.

§4 Colloquial English — Talking About Screen Time 4 natural phrases · Digital life context · Used daily by native speakers ⏱ 5 MIN
"I check my phone without thinking."
Describes automatic, unconscious behaviour. "Without thinking" = without awareness or intention. The most honest way to describe habitual phone use.
"I was in the middle of a conversation and I checked my phone without thinking. I didn't even realise."
"I need to set some limits."
Natural way to talk about reducing usage. "Set limits" = establish boundaries. More natural than "I want to use my phone less." Very common in wellness conversations.
"My screen time was 7 hours yesterday. I need to set some limits — this isn't healthy."
"I can't focus for more than 5 minutes."
Describes reduced attention span — a direct consequence of heavy phone use. Honest and relatable. Pairs well with passive: "My attention was shortened by…"
"Since I started working from home, I can't focus for more than 5 minutes without checking something."
"I keep getting pulled back in."
Describes the app engagement loop — the feeling that you can't stop, even when you want to. "Keep -ing" = repeated action. Passive-adjacent: you are pulled, not the puller.
"I said I'd stop at 10pm. But I keep getting pulled back in — one more notification, one more video."
💡 Quick Drill A colleague says they checked their phone 80 times yesterday. What do you say? Push for: "80 times? That's about once every 12 minutes. Are those notifications designed by the app? Or are you checking out of habit?" — note how "designed by the app" is passive, and "out of habit" connects to today's vocabulary.
