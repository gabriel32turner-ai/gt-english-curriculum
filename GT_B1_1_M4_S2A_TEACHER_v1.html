§1 Warm-Up — Your Screen Time Check Activate digital life vocabulary · Connect to real phone habits ⏱ 5 MIN
📱 How many hours a day do you spend on your phone? Do you know your weekly screen time?
→ T→S: Ask everyone. Compare numbers. Who has the highest? Note answers on the board.
💼 What do you use your phone for most — work, social media, or entertainment?
→ T→S: Push for specifics — WhatsApp for work? Instagram after dinner? YouTube before sleep?
🔄 Have you ever tried to reduce your screen time? What happened?
→ S→S: Pair up. Push for story — "I tried for 3 days and then…"
😰 If your phone was taken away for 24 hours, what would you feel?
→ S→S: Answers reveal dependency level. Connect to today's passive grammar: "I would feel disconnected…"

§2 Speaking — Is Your Screen Time a Problem? 3 rounds · Passive voice emerging naturally · Model B1.1 answer ⏱ 8 MIN
Session Topic
Screen Time — How Much Is Too Much?
Round 1 — 60s · T→S
Describe your typical day on your phone — morning to night. When do you pick it up first? When do you put it down last?
"My phone is picked up before I even get out of bed — the alarm is set on it, so it's right there. Then messages are checked, Instagram is opened... by 9am I've already spent 40 minutes on it. At night it's the same thing in reverse."
Round 2 — 90s · S→S
Your partner: When is screen time a problem? When is it OK? Are there situations where you should disconnect completely?
"I think it becomes a problem when it's used automatically — when you pick it up without thinking why. For work, fine. For staying in touch, fine. But when dinner is being eaten while Instagram is being scrolled — that's a problem."
Round 3 — 90s · Class
Should companies be required by law to show users their screen time data? Should apps be designed with usage limits built in?
"I think data should be shown clearly — not buried. But limits shouldn't be imposed by law. That said, these apps are designed to maximize time spent — not to help you. So maybe some regulation is needed. The question is: who decides what's too much?"
Model Answer — Color-Coded
"My phone is used for about 5 hours a day — mostly for work, but also Instagram and YouTube. Notifications are sent every few minutes and they're designed to bring you back. I tried to reduce my usage last month — I set limits, but they were bypassed after 2 days. What I've realized is that the apps are built to be checked constantly. It's not really about willpower."
■ Passive Voice ■ Target Vocabulary ■ Colloquial Phrase
📘 Context Note Notice how the model answer uses passive voice naturally: "notifications are sent", "apps are built", "limits were bypassed." This is intentional — tech language is full of passive voice because it de-centers the agent (the company). Help students see that passive in tech is often a rhetorical choice, not just grammar.

§4 Colloquial English — Talking About Screen Time 4 natural phrases · Digital life context · Used daily by native speakers ⏱ 5 MIN
"I check my phone without thinking."
Describes automatic, unconscious behaviour. "Without thinking" = without awareness or intention. The most honest way to describe habitual phone use.
"I was in the middle of a conversation and I checked my phone without thinking. I didn't even realise."
"I need to set some limits."
Natural way to talk about reducing usage. "Set limits" = establish boundaries. More natural than "I want to use my phone less." Very common in wellness conversations.
"My screen time was 7 hours yesterday. I need to set some limits — this isn't healthy."
"I can't focus for more than 5 minutes."
Describes reduced attention span — a direct consequence of heavy phone use. Honest and relatable. Pairs well with passive: "My attention was shortened by…"
"Since I started working from home, I can't focus for more than 5 minutes without checking something."
"I keep getting pulled back in."
Describes the app engagement loop — the feeling that you can't stop, even when you want to. "Keep -ing" = repeated action. Passive-adjacent: you are pulled, not the puller.
"I said I'd stop at 10pm. But I keep getting pulled back in — one more notification, one more video."
💡 Quick Drill A colleague says they checked their phone 80 times yesterday. What do you say? Push for: "80 times? That's about once every 12 minutes. Are those notifications designed by the app? Or are you checking out of habit?" — note how "designed by the app" is passive, and "out of habit" connects to today's vocabulary.
§5 Grammar — Passive Voice in Context Technology language · Referential approach · 2 activities + Poll ⏱ 10 MIN
📘 Referential Teaching Note Today's passive focus: technology is FULL of passive voice because companies don't want to say "WE collect your data" — they say "data is collected." This is intentional. Help students SEE this pattern — it changes how they read tech agreements, app notifications, and news about AI. The passive hides the agent. That's the lesson.
StructureFormScreen Time Example
Present Passiveis/are + past participle"Screen time is tracked automatically."
Past Passivewas/were + past participle"App limits were bypassed in 2 minutes."
Passive with agentis/are + past part + by"Usage data is collected by the platform."
Negative Passiveis/are + not + past part"Sleep is not improved by late screen time."
Error — missing beverb alone✗ "This app designed to distract."
✅ "This app was designed to distract."
💡 The rule that matters most: Passive always needs "be." "Screen time is tracked." / "Limits were bypassed." Without "be," it's an error — the most common mistake at B1.1.
⚠️ Correct immediately:
✗ "This app designed to distract you." → ✅ "This app was designed to distract you."
✗ "Usage data collected every day." → ✅ "Usage data is collected every day."
✗ "My sleep not improved by this." → ✅ "My sleep is not improved by this."
⚡ Quick Poll — Earn 🎯 badge
1. "App limits ______ after I set them, but they were easy to bypass."
✅ Correct! "were set" — Past Passive. "Were" = past of "be" + "set" = past participle.
❌ "Set" alone is missing the "be" verb. → "App limits were set after I set them."
❌ "Are setting" is present continuous active — passive needs: were + past participle.
2. "Notifications ______ every few minutes to bring you back."
✅ Correct! "are sent" — Present Passive. "Notifications" is plural → "are". Agent (the app) is deliberately hidden.
❌ "Sent" alone is missing "are". Passive = be + past participle.
❌ "Is sent" uses singular "is" — "notifications" is plural: use "are sent".
3. "My sleep quality ______ significantly since I stopped using my phone at night."
✅ Correct! "was improved" — Past Passive. "Was" = past "be" + "improved" = past participle of "improve".
❌ "Improves" is Present Simple active — the passive better shows a change was caused by an action.
❌ "Improved" (Past Simple active) is possible but "was improved" shows the change was caused by stopping phone use.

§6 Listening — Sarah's Screen Time Wake-Up Call Sarah (AmE) · Speed 0.82 · Passive voice throughout · MC + T/F ⏱ 8 MIN
Sarah's Screen Time Wake-Up Call
B1.1 · M4 · S2A · Voice: Sarah (American English) · Speed: 0.82
Six weeks ago, I looked at my screen time report and nearly had a heart attack. Seven hours and twenty minutes. Per day. I couldn't believe it — that's almost a third of my waking hours. I asked myself: when was this habit formed? I don't even remember deciding to use my phone this much.

The thing is, these apps are designed to keep you coming back. Notifications are sent at the perfect moment. The content is curated by an algorithm that knows exactly what you want to see. And the limits I tried to set? They were bypassed in about two minutes. Turns out, willpower alone isn't enough.

So I did something more radical. My phone was placed in a drawer from 9pm to 7am every night for one month. No exceptions. And the results were surprising. My sleep was improved dramatically — I went from 5 hours to almost 7. My focus at work was better. And this is the strange part: I was less anxious. The constant checking was eliminated, and I felt calmer.

The habit hasn't been completely broken. But my relationship with my phone has changed. I'm more conscious now. I choose when to check it — rather than being pulled in automatically.
📎 ElevenLabs: Voice — Sarah (American English) · Speed 0.82 · Pitch 1.0 · B1.1 M4 S2A

§7 Production — Your Screen Time Story Opinion meter · 4 speaking prompts · 90-sec challenge · Hot Seat Dice ⏱ 12 MIN
📊 Opinion Meter — Where do you stand?
"Smartphones do more harm than good."
💬 How to run this: Students answer using passive voice. Partner marks: (1) any missing "be" verb in passive, (2) any incorrect tense. Correct after each response. Q1 = warm-up, Q2–Q3 = real, Q4 = critical thinking.
Question 1 — T→S · Passive Warm-Up
Describe your screen time in 3 passive sentences: "My attention was affected by…", "My sleep is impacted by…", "My phone is used for…"
💡 Push for specific details — what app, what time, what effect? The passive makes it sound analytical, not defensive.
Question 2 — S→S · Partner Question
Ask your partner: "What was the last thing your phone distracted you from?" Listen — can you identify passive structures in their answer?
💡 Model: "I was distracted from a meeting by a WhatsApp notification. The meeting was interrupted and I lost focus."
Question 3 — T→S · Hypothetical
If your screen time was shared publicly with your colleagues every Monday morning, what would change? Would you feel embarrassed? Motivated?
💡 Note how the question uses passive: "was shared." Push for: "My habits would be changed…" / "I would be embarrassed if…"
Question 4 — Class · Critical Thinking
Is it the user's fault — or were these apps designed to be impossible to stop? Where does personal responsibility end and corporate responsibility begin?
💡 Push students to use: "These apps were built to…" / "Users are being targeted by…" — passive as a critical analytical tool.

🎯 This week — use these 3 phrases
3 Phrases Starting Tomorrow
PHRASE 1
"I check my phone without thinking."
Use this when describing your own automatic behaviour — next time you catch yourself reaching for your phone without a reason. It's honest, relatable, and immediately understood by native speakers.
PHRASE 2
"This was designed to keep you coming back."
Passive voice used critically — about apps, social media, algorithms. Use it next time someone complains about being unable to stop scrolling. Sounds analytical and shows B1+ grammar naturally.
PHRASE 3
"I need to disconnect."
Simple and powerful. Use it at the end of a workday, before dinner, or when overwhelmed by notifications. Add a reason: "I need to disconnect — my attention is gone." Two words that show digital self-awareness.
