§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

Immigration — What Would You Do?

Q1  Empathy First
Before we discuss immigration as a topic, think of one thing you would need if you had to move to a completely different country tomorrow. What would be hardest?
Q2  Work
Have you ever worked with someone from another country? What did that person bring to your team that was different or valuable?
Q3  Mexico Context
Many workers from other Mexican states move to the Bajío region for manufacturing jobs. Is that also immigration? What does integration look like in your company or city?
Q4  Opinion
If a highly qualified engineer from another country wanted to work at your company, would your team welcome them? Be honest. What barriers might exist?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Complete this sentence out loud: “If I had to move to another country for work, I would…” — Say it. Don’t overthink. The grammar comes naturally.
This is the GT-designed empathy sequence: build personal connection BEFORE asking for opinions. Q1 is always first — it generates empathy, which makes Q4 more nuanced. Learners who start with “what would I need?” give more thoughtful answers to “should companies hire immigrants?” Expect Mexico-specific insights on internal migration in Q3 — this is a real, lived experience for many Bajío workers.
Immigration is sensitive. If anyone in the room has personal immigration experience (or family experience), that’s a gift — invite it, don’t ignore it. The Quick Win forces Second Conditional use naturally — learners don’t even notice they’re using grammar.
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“If countries truly valued immigration, would diversity in the workplace still be a challenge?”

Immigration shapes industries. In México, foreign investment from companies like BMW, Audi, and Toyota brought workers, managers, and engineers from dozens of countries to the Bajío region. Some integrated smoothly. Others faced language barriers, cultural misunderstandings, and prejudice. At the same time, millions of Mexicans have built lives abroad. Today’s session asks: what does real integration look like — and what stands in the way?

Make this local and real: BMW in San Luis, Audi in San José Chiapa, Hella in Celaya — all brought international workers. Ask: “Has anyone worked directly with an expat manager? What was that like?” This grounds the topic before vocabulary and grammar begin. 90 seconds pair discussion, then 3-4 share.

§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Second Conditional — Extended Use

⚠ REAL SITUATION
A colleague says: “Honestly, if I were an immigrant trying to get a job here, I don’t know what I’d do. The system is complicated.”

How would you respond? How do you express sympathy, hypothetical advice, and opinion about a situation that isn’t happening to you right now? Second Conditional is exactly the tool.
✎ THE TOOL — SECOND CONDITIONAL IN DEBATE & EMPATHY
If + past simple  →  would/could/might + base verb
At B1.2, we expand: would (certain result) • could (possible result) • might (less certain result)
ModalUseExample
wouldCertain hypothetical resultIf companies removed bias from hiring, diversity would increase.
couldPossible or optional resultIf she adapted to the culture, she could become a team leader.
mightUncertain or speculative resultIf there were better integration programs, immigrants might feel less isolated.
⚠ Remember: IF clause = past simple. Main clause = would/could/might + base verb. Never “would” in the IF clause.
ACTIVITY A — Choose the correct form

2. If I _____ to another country, I _____ very homesick at first.

A. could integrate / offered

Carlos Mendez on Belonging

OPINION METER — Before you listen, vote:
“If companies in México hired more internationally, the quality of work and innovation would improve.”

Carlos Mendez:

I want to tell you about a client of mine. An engineer. Brilliant. He came from Colombia to work at a plant in Guanajuato. He had fifteen years of experience. His technical knowledge was exceptional. But in his first year, he was invisible. Not because he couldn’t do the job. Because nobody took the time to include him.

If his manager had assigned him a mentor in the first week, he would have integrated in months, not years. If the team had invited him to social events, he might have built the relationships that make work feel like work, not survival. Instead, he spent 18 months feeling like a foreigner in a room full of people he worked with every day.

Here’s what I know from coaching: the burden of integration should not fall only on the immigrant. If companies designed real inclusion programs, the contribution of international talent would transform their results.

And yet — I also believe in personal responsibility. If I moved to Germany tomorrow, I would learn German. I would study the culture. I would adapt. Both sides have to move.

At the end of the day, diversity is not a gift that arrives automatically. It’s a result of deliberate, consistent action — from companies, from teams, and from individuals.

§SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

Build the Argument

RECALL
What happened to Carlos’s Colombian client during his first year? Tell it in your own words.
Grammar hint: Use simple past — “He worked at… / He struggled because… / He felt…”
EMPATHY
If you were that Colombian engineer, how would you feel after 18 months? What would you do next?
Grammar hint: Second Conditional — “If I were in that situation, I would… / I might…”
WORK CONTEXT
Does your company have any integration or onboarding process for new employees? If you designed one, what would you include?
Grammar hint: “If I designed an onboarding program, I would include… because…”
DEBATE
Carlos says both sides must move — the company AND the immigrant. Do you agree? Is there one side that should do more?
Grammar hint: Use “could” for suggestions — “Companies could… / Immigrants could…”
HYPOTHETICAL
If your company offered you a 2-year position in Germany, Japan, or the USA — which would you choose and why? What would you need to adapt?
Grammar hint: “If I chose _____, I would need to… / I might struggle with…”
CHALLENGE
Someone says: “Hiring foreigners takes jobs from local people.” Use session vocabulary to build a counterargument. Aim for 60 seconds.
Grammar hint: “If companies only hired locally, they might miss… / At the end of the day, diversity…”
Q5 is a personal dream-scenario question — high engagement, lower stakes. Let learners run with it. Q6 is the production challenge: require learners to use at least 3 session vocabulary words AND one M4 colloquial phrase in their counterargument. This is authentic argumentative production at B1.2 level. Time them at 60 seconds and give peer feedback on vocabulary use, not just grammar.
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“If I were in your situation, I might…”
Use it with a colleague who is facing a challenge — especially useful when you want to show empathy without being too direct or prescriptive.
“At the end of the day, both sides need to move.”
Use it to close a debate or disagreement by acknowledging that responsibility is shared. Works in team meetings and performance conversations.
“That’s something we could do differently.”
Use it to suggest change without criticising directly. “Could” makes proposals sound collaborative, not confrontational — essential in cross-cultural work environments.
