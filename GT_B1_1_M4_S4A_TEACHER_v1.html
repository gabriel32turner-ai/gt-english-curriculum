§2 Speaking — The Digital Miscommunication Problem 3 rounds · Passive voice emerging naturally · Model B1.1 answer ⏱ 8 MIN
Session Topic
Why Does Written Communication Cause So Many Problems?
Round 1 — 60s · T→S
Describe a time you were misunderstood in a text — or misread someone else's message.
"Last month, a message I sent was misread by my manager. I wrote 'Fine, let's do it that way' — it was interpreted as sarcasm."
Round 2 — 90s · S→S
Ask your partner: What's your communication rule for difficult conversations — text or call?
"My rule is: if it's sensitive, it should be handled by phone. Tone is completely lost in written messages — especially when context is removed."
Round 3 — 90s · Class
Should work conflicts be resolved by phone, not text? Why?
"I believe conflicts should always be resolved by phone or face-to-face — not text. The more we communicate digitally, the more we misunderstand each other."
Model Answer — Color-Coded
"Last month, a message I sent was misread by my manager. I wrote 'Fine, let's do it that way' — and it was interpreted as sarcasm, not agreement. A meeting was called, feelings were hurt, and the whole thing was caused by three words without context. I've learned that tone is completely lost in written messages. Difficult conversations should be handled by phone or face-to-face — not text. The irony is that the more we communicate digitally, the more we misunderstand each other."
■ Passive Voice ■ Target Vocabulary ■ Colloquial Phrase
📘 Context Note Notice how the model answer uses passive voice naturally: "was misread", "was interpreted", "was called", "were hurt", "was caused", "should be handled." When things go wrong in communication, passive voice appears because we describe outcomes without assigning blame directly — this is exactly how diplomatic professional English works. Help students see the pattern.

§4 Colloquial English — Talking About Digital Miscommunication 4 natural phrases · Professional de-escalation · Used daily by native speakers ⏱ 5 MIN
"That didn't come across the way I meant it."
When a message was misunderstood. "Come across" = how something is perceived. Very natural for apologizing without saying sorry directly — professional and diplomatic.
"I sent that report feedback and my colleague was upset. That didn't come across the way I meant it."
"I think we need to talk — not text."
When switching to voice communication. The pause "—" adds emphasis. Signals the conversation has become too complex for written messages. Direct but not aggressive.
"This conversation is getting complicated. I think we need to talk — not text."
"I was being sarcastic — but you can't tell in writing."
Explaining miscommunication after the fact. "You can't tell" = it's impossible to know. Very common when tone is lost in text — honest and self-aware.
"When I wrote 'great idea', I was being sarcastic — but you can't tell in writing."
"Let me clarify what I meant."
Fixing a misunderstanding. "Let me" softens the correction — it sounds helpful, not defensive. Standard professional phrase when you realize your message was misread.
"I think my email was misread. Let me clarify what I meant — I was agreeing, not criticizing."
💡 Quick Drill Your colleague sent "OK." (with a period) to your proposal. How do you feel? What do you say next? Push for: "I think that was misread as negative — let me clarify." §5 Grammar — Passive Voice: Communication Context Diplomatic passive · Referential approach · Modal passives · 2 activities + Poll ⏱ 10 MIN
📘 Referential Teaching Note Today's focus: passive voice in communication contexts. When things go wrong in business communication, passive voice is used to describe problems without blaming — "The message was misread" vs. "You misread the message." This is diplomatic and professional. Help students use passive to de-escalate: it removes the accuser and the accused, leaving only the problem to solve.
StructureFormCommunication Example
Present Passiveis/are + past participle"Tone is often misread in text messages."
Past Passivewas/were + past participle"The email was sent to the wrong person."
Passive for blame reductionwas/were + past participle"The message was misunderstood." (vs. "You misunderstood")
Modal Passiveshould/can + be + past part"Conflicts should be resolved by phone, not text."
Error — missing "be"verb alone✗ "The tone misread."
✅ "The tone was misread."
💡 The rule that matters most: Passive always needs "be." "The message was misread." / "Conflicts should be resolved." Without "be," it's an error — the most common mistake at B1.1. For modal passives: should/can + be + past participle — never skip the "be."
⚠️ Correct immediately:
✗ "The tone misread as rude." → ✅ "The tone was misread as rude."
✗ "Conflicts should resolved by phone." → ✅ "Conflicts should be resolved by phone."
✗ "The email interpreted as a rejection." → ✅ "The email was interpreted as a rejection."
⚡ Quick Poll — 30 pts · Earn 🎯 badge at 30
1. "My email ______ as aggressive, but I didn't mean it that way."
✅ Correct! "was interpreted" — Past Passive. "Was" = past of "be" + "interpreted" = past participle. The passive hides who did the interpreting — diplomatic and professional.
❌ "Interpreted" alone is missing "be." Passive = was + past participle → "was interpreted." This is the most common B1.1 error.
❌ "Is interpreting" is Present Continuous active. We need Past Passive because this happened in the past: was + interpreted.
2. "Difficult conversations should ______ face-to-face, not by text."
✅ Correct! "should be handled" — Modal Passive. should + be + past participle. This is the structure for professional recommendations — it removes who is responsible.
❌ "Handle" is active and missing "be." Modal passive = should + be + past participle → "should be handled."
❌ "Be handling" is continuous — we need the simple form: should be + past participle → "should be handled."
3. "The misunderstanding ______ with a 5-minute phone call."
✅ Correct! "was resolved" — Past Passive. A misunderstanding from the past, fixed (by someone, unnamed). The passive focuses on the result, not who fixed it.
❌ "Resolved" alone is missing "was." Passive = was + past participle → "was resolved." Without "was," it reads as active past simple (incomplete meaning).
❌ "Is resolved" is Present Passive — but the context describes a past event. Use Past Passive: "was resolved."

§6 Listening — Rodrigo's Inbox Disaster Sarah (AmE) · Speed 0.82 · Passive voice throughout · MC + T/F ⏱ 8 MIN
Rodrigo's Inbox Disaster
B1.1 · M4 · S4A · Voice: Sarah (American English) · Speed: 0.82
Last Tuesday, I made a mistake that still makes me cringe. I sent a feedback email about a colleague's presentation — and it was not received well. At all.

What I wrote was: 'The data was presented clearly, but the structure could be improved.' Seems fine, right? But what was read was: 'Your presentation was bad and you don't know what you're doing.'

The email was forwarded to the team leader. A meeting was called. I was asked to explain myself. The whole thing was caused by two things: I didn't add any context, and I used passive voice in a way that felt cold and distancing — which was ironic, because I was trying to be diplomatic.

What I've learned is this. Feedback should never be given by email. It's too easy for tone to be lost, for context to be removed, for meaning to be misread. If criticism is needed, it should be delivered face-to-face — or at minimum, by phone.

Now I follow a rule: if a message takes more than three drafts to write, it means I should call instead. Written communication is fast — but it's not always clear. And clarity should always be chosen over speed.

§7 Production — Your Communication Policy Opinion meter · 4 speaking prompts · 90-sec challenge · Hot Seat Dice ⏱ 12 MIN
📊 Opinion Meter — Where do you stand?
"Digital communication does more harm than good at work."
💬 How to run this: Students use passive voice throughout. Partner marks: (1) any missing "be" verb in passive, (2) any modal passive error ("should be resolved" not "should resolved"). Correct after each response. Q1 = structured policy, Q2 = personal experience peer, Q3 = hypothetical, Q4 = critical thinking.
Question 1 — T→S · Passive Warm-Up
Describe a digital communication rule for your team using passive: "Conflicts should be resolved by…", "Feedback should be given by…", "Urgent messages should be sent by…"
💡 Push for specific rules from their real workplace. The passive sounds formal and policy-like — exactly how companies write communication guidelines.
Question 2 — S→S · Partner Question
Ask your partner: Have you ever been misunderstood in writing? What was misread?
💡 Listen for passive: "I was misunderstood…" / "The message was misread as…" Note any missing "be" verbs and correct after the student finishes.
Question 3 — T→S · Hypothetical
If all work communication was face-to-face only — no email, no text — what would change in your company?
💡 Note the question uses past simple hypothetical: "was face-to-face only." Push for modal passives in answers: "Decisions would be made faster…" / "Conflicts would be resolved more easily…"
Question 4 — Class · Critical Thinking
Should companies be required to set rules about when email can and can't be used?
💡 Push for: "Rules should be set by…" / "Email should not be used for…" / "Feedback should never be given by…" — modal passives as analytical tool for policy discussion.

🎯 This week — use these 3 phrases
3 Phrases Starting Tomorrow
PHRASE 1
"That didn't come across the way I meant it."
Professional de-escalation. Use it the next time a message you wrote is misunderstood — it apologizes without saying sorry and keeps the conversation professional. Shows B1+ vocabulary and emotional intelligence.
PHRASE 2
"Conflicts should be resolved by phone, not text."
Modal passive in context. Use it whenever a work conversation turns tense over text. It's assertive, professional, and shows you can use grammar strategically — the mark of a confident B1+ speaker.
PHRASE 3
"Let me clarify what I meant."
Fix a misunderstanding professionally. Use it any time your message was misread — in email, WhatsApp, or a meeting. "Let me" is confident and helpful. It turns a potential conflict into a constructive conversation.
