Warm-Up — Reviewing Will & Surfacing New Uses
Activate S1A knowledge · Introduce promises and predictions gap · Connect to real client situations
⏳ 5–8 MIN
💡 Pedagogical: Students know will for offers and spontaneous decisions from S1A. Open today by asking: "Can you use will to make a promise?" Let students try. Most will feel something is different about promise-will (stronger, more personal commitment). Don't explain yet — create curiosity. Then ask: "Can you use will to say what you think will happen in the future?" That's prediction. Both uses extend will naturally from S1A.
🔄 S1A Review: Last session you learned will for offers and decisions. Give your partner TWO examples you actually remember from that session. Can you do it without looking at notes?
🤝 Promise gap: Your client is angry about a late delivery. You need to reassure them. Try saying: "I _____ make sure this never happens again." — What's different about this "will" compared to an offer? How does it feel?
📈 Prediction moment: A colleague asks: "Do you think sales will be good next month?" How do you answer? Try: "I think it _____ be…" — What word do you put there? Is it the same will?
⚡ Teacher note: Listen for any spontaneous use of "will" or "going to." If students say "going to be good," ask: "Did you see data, or is it your feeling/opinion?" That gap — evidence vs. belief — is today's going-to vs. will contrast in §3. Bookmark it.
⚡ Quick Win — 90 seconds
Think of ONE time at work when you (or a colleague) made a promise to a client. What was the promise? Did you keep it? Tell your partner in English — use "I'll…" or "We'll…"

§3
Grammar — Will for Promises & Predictions
Two functions · Adverbs of probability · Will vs Going To evidence contrast · Error correction · Drag sort
⏳ 20–25 MIN
💡 Pedagogical: The key insight for today: Will has TWO distinct functions beyond offers. (1) PROMISE: always first person (I/We), strong personal commitment, past context of failure or worry. (2) PREDICTION: any person, based on opinion/belief — NOT evidence. Contrast with "going to" which uses visible evidence. Don't overload — teach the table, then adverbs, then contrast. That's three focused punches.
Core Problem
Students often use will for everything — they don't know when a promise feels stronger than a prediction, or when going to is more natural than will for future events. Today we draw those lines clearly.

⚠️ Key contrast: Will = opinion ("I think…"). Going to = evidence ("Look at this data…"). In business English this matters: going to sounds more data-driven; will sounds like a personal belief or prediction. Neither is wrong — context decides.

📊 Quick Poll: Laura tells a worried client: "I'm confident the new product will attract mid-sized clients." — What function does will have here?
B is correct. "I'm confident" signals an opinion-based prediction — not evidence (which would need "going to"), not a personal commitment (which would need first person + action).
B es correcto. "I'm confident" indica predicción basada en opinión personal, no en evidencia visible.
ERROR CORRECTION — spot the mistake
Find the errors. Think before you look at the answers.

1. "I will probably won't be in the office tomorrow."
2. "Look at this data — I think the sales will be great." (context: speaker has a detailed report showing 40% growth)
3. "They will definitely not accepts the offer."
4. "We going to deliver it by Friday, I promise."
1. ❌ "I will probably won't…" — Can't use both will and won't. Correct: "I probably won't be in the office tomorrow."
2. ⚠️ With hard data/report, "going to" is more natural: "The sales are going to be great" — will is possible but less precise.
3. ❌ "accepts" → base form: "They definitely won't accept the offer."
4. ❌ "going to" + "I promise" = contradiction. Spontaneous promises use will: "We'll deliver it by Friday, I promise."

Listening — Client Review Monologue
Single speaker · Laura Mendoza, Account Manager · Promises + predictions · 4 MCQ + 4 TF
⏳ 20–25 MIN
💡 Pre-listening: Before playing, ask: "How do you think an account manager opens a quarterly review with a worried client?" Let 2–3 students guess. Then say: "Listen for TWO types of will — promises and predictions. Count them." Play once for gist, then again for the MCQ.
Laura Mendoza — Quarterly Client Review
Account Manager · AmE Sarah voice · 0.82× speed · ~190 words
🔓 ElevenLabs TTS — Voice: Sarah (AmE) · Stability 0.55 · Similarity 0.78 · Speed 0.82
Good morning, Mr. Chen. Thank you for joining this quarterly review. I want to start with our commitments. First — I'll personally oversee the final delivery. I promise you it won't be delayed again. Our team will make sure the documentation is ready by next Thursday, and I'll send you a confirmation email as soon as it's done. Now — what about the results? I think the next quarter will definitely be stronger. Based on the data, revenue will probably increase by 15 to 18 percent. The new product line will likely attract mid-sized clients, and I'm confident that our customer satisfaction scores will improve significantly. Will there be challenges? Probably. But I won't let those challenges affect your account. I guarantee our team will respond to any issue within 24 hours — no exceptions. One more thing: I predict this partnership will grow considerably over the next 12 months. We won't let you down, Mr. Chen. That's a promise.
🔎 Analysis task (after 2nd listen): Ask students to highlight: (1) every "I'll / We'll" promise sentence in blue, (2) every prediction sentence in yellow. Count: there should be 5+ promises and 4+ predictions. Compare with a partner — did you agree on every sentence?

Role-Play — Client Review Call
3 rounds: promises · predictions with adverbs · full 2-min call
⏳ 18–22 MIN
🎭 Setup: Student A = Account Manager. Student B = worried client (Mr./Ms. Chen). Teacher circulates and listens for: correct will use in promises (first person), appropriate adverbs in predictions, won't in negative promises. After Round 3, ask: "How did it feel to switch from promise to prediction tone?" That metacognitive reflection is valuable.
Round 1 — 3 min · Promises only
Your client is anxious about three things: late delivery, missing documentation, and slow response times. Make exactly 3 promises — one for each concern. Use: "I'll / We'll / I won't / I promise I'll…"
Starter: "Mr. Chen, I understand your concerns. First, I'll personally…"
"Mr. Chen, I understand your concerns. First — I'll personally oversee the delivery to make sure it arrives on time. Second — I won't let the documentation be incomplete again. Our team will have everything ready by Thursday. And third — I promise I'll respond to any question within 24 hours. You have my word."
Round 2 — 3 min · Predictions with adverbs
Now shift to the future outlook. Make 4 predictions about next quarter: one with "definitely", one with "probably", one with "likely", and one with "I'm confident that". Don't make promises — only predictions.
Starter: "Now, let me share what I think the next quarter will look like…"
"Now, let me share what I think the next quarter will look like. Revenue will definitely be stronger — I'm very confident in our pipeline. The new product line will probably attract at least 10 new clients. Our delivery process will likely improve by 20 percent. And I'm confident that your satisfaction score will be significantly higher at our next review."
Round 3 — 5 min · Full client review call
Combine both skills. Open a 2-minute client review: (1) open professionally, (2) make at least 2 promises, (3) make at least 2 predictions with adverbs, (4) close with a strong promise. No notes — full production.
You can model on Laura's monologue or create your own context. Client can ask questions between sections.
Teacher coaching cues:
→ If student says "going to" for a promise: "Good — but a promise sounds stronger with 'will'. Try again?"
→ If student uses no adverbs: "Add a probably or definitely to soften or strengthen your prediction."
→ If student confuses function: "Is that what you think will happen, or what you commit to doing? Those feel different."
→ Strong close: student should end with a clear won't or will promise, not a prediction.

Teacher Gold Tips — S2A Wrap
  • If students still confuse promise and prediction: ask "Who is acting?" — promise = I/We act. Prediction = anyone/anything happens.
  • The adverb ladder (definitely → certainly → probably → maybe) is a tool for hedging in business. Encourage students to use it to sound natural, not binary.
  • Going to vs will for predictions: tell students "In a meeting, if you have a report, say going to. If it's your gut feeling, say will." Simplifies the contrast.
  • S2B preview: conditional offers ("If you approve by Friday, we'll include the extra module") are the next natural step — plant the seed in the final 2 minutes.
