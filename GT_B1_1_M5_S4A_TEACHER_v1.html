🧡 Warm-Up — Mental Health Awareness

5–8 min
⚠ Teacher note — Safe Space Setup (read before class): Mental health is the most sensitive topic in this module. Some students may have personal experience with anxiety, depression, or burnout. Do NOT push anyone to share personal details. Frame the session from the start: "Today we are talking about mental health at work as a professional topic — how the conversation is changing, and what we can say in English. You never need to share anything personal." This framing protects students and keeps the session professionally anchored.
Module 5 · Session 4A — Mental Health Awareness · Is it easier now to talk about this at work?
🔁 Module 5 so far: S1A — Reported statements · S2A — Reported questions · S3A — Combining both in a narrative. Today we continue reporting what people say and ask — now in the context of mental health conversations at work, where the exact words people use matter enormously.
Q1 — The shift
Five years ago, mental health was rarely discussed at work in Mexico. Has that changed in your experience? What caused the shift?
Q2 — The workplace
Does your company take mental health seriously? Is it something people actually talk about, or is it still a taboo subject where you work?
Q3 — The language
In Spanish, how do you say you are struggling mentally at work — without sounding weak? What words do people use? How would you say that in English?
Q4 — The courage
Have you ever asked a colleague how they were really doing — and meant it? Did they open up? What made that conversation possible?
⚡ Quick Win — 20 seconds
Say this out loud: "My colleague told me she had been feeling anxious for weeks, but she said she hadn't felt comfortable asking for support until now."
This is B1.1 Reported Speech with sensitive content handled professionally. That is today's target.
Teacher note — Q3 is your linguistic gold: Asking how people talk about mental health struggle in Spanish surfaces the exact vocabulary gap you will fill in §3. Students often say "estresado," "agotado," or "me siento mal" — and don't know the English equivalents. Write their Spanish words on the board, leave a column blank, and fill it in together during §3.

Q4 framing: If a student shares a personal story, thank them and redirect: "That's a powerful example. Notice how you described it — let's use that as our sentence model for §5."
Cultural note — Mexico & mental health: Traditionally, mental health struggles are private in Mexican workplace culture — particularly for men in manufacturing environments. The generational shift is real but uneven. Younger employees (Gen Z, early Millennials) are far more open. Use this gap as content: "Do you think there is a generational difference at your company?" This generates natural, authentic discussion.

🗣️ Speaking Topic — Mental Health at Work

8 min

Is it easier now to talk about mental health at work? Why or why not?

This topic generates the most personal and authentic discussion in the module. Approach it professionally: focus on what people say and ask, not on personal disclosures. Use Reported Speech to keep a safe distance while still talking from real experience.

Mental Health at Work · 3 Discussion Rounds
T→S Round 1
Tell me: do you think mental health is treated as seriously as physical health at your company? Give one concrete example — something that happened, something someone said.
S↔S Round 2
Ask your partner: "Has anyone ever asked you how you were really doing at work — and you felt it was genuine?" Report their answer: "My partner said that someone had asked them..."
Class Round 3
Class debate: "Companies have a responsibility to actively protect employee mental health, not just react when someone breaks down." Agree or disagree? What should companies actually do?

Mental health at work — natural B1.1 response:

"I think the conversation has changed, but slowly. A colleague of mine told me she had been feeling anxious for months before she finally talked to anyone. She said she had not felt comfortable because she was afraid of stigma. People talk about it more now, especially after the pandemic — but I am not sure companies have really changed their structures. She asked me if I thought things were improving, and honestly, I said I was not sure. I think we are in a transition. It's okay to ask for help — but saying that is easier than believing it."

Note: S4 vocabulary · Reported question integrated · S4 colloquial phrases

Teacher note — Round 2 Reported Speech: The double-layer (“my partner said that someone had asked them”) is a genuine B1.1 stretch. If students produce it, mark it as excellent. If not, model it after the round: write the chain on the board — Direct → Partner's report → Your report of their report. Three levels. Visual representation matters here.

💬 Colloquial English — Mental Health Phrases

5 min
3 natural phrases for talking about mental health — professionally and authentically
"It's okay to ask for help."
When to use
When encouraging a colleague or acknowledging that you yourself needed support. "I know it feels hard to say something, but it's okay to ask for help — that is what the team is here for."
"People talk about it more now."
When to use
When describing the cultural shift around mental health. "Five years ago, nobody discussed burnout openly. People talk about it more now — especially after the pandemic changed everything."
"I take it one day at a time."
When to use
When describing how you manage difficulty without oversharing. "It's not easy, but I take it one day at a time and try not to worry about next month." Understated, authentic, and very natural in English.
"How are you really doing?"
When to use
When you want to check in genuinely, not just exchange pleasantries. Stress the word "really" — it signals that you want an honest answer. A small phrase that opens big conversations.
Teacher note — Colloquial: "How are you really doing?" is the session's most actionable phrase. It is something students can use in English this week — with a colleague, in a team meeting, or with their manager. Drill the stress pattern: "How are you REALLY doing?" vs. "How are you really DOING?" — the first puts emphasis on the sincerity, the second sounds more formal. The first is correct for casual, warm use.

Cultural note: In English, "How are you?" is a greeting, not a real question. "How are you REALLY doing?" signals that you actually want to know. Students who understand this distinction sound culturally fluent, not just linguistically correct.
Retention layer: Ask: "Which of these 4 phrases could you use in your workplace in the next 7 days?" Take 3 answers. Then: "Who will you say it to?" Making it concrete and personal is what separates phrases that get used from phrases that get forgotten.

⚡ Grammar — Reported Speech: Sensitive Contexts & Precise Reporting

12–15 min
Grammar · Why the exact reported form matters when the topic is sensitive
🎯 Real Work Situation

Your colleague confided in you that she had been struggling with anxiety. Later, your manager asks what happened in the meeting. You need to report what she said — accurately, professionally, and with care. The wrong reported form can distort what she actually said.

Direct: "I have been feeling anxious for months. I haven't talked to anyone because I was afraid."

How do you report this to your manager without misrepresenting her?

🔧 The Tool — Precision in Reported Speech

In sensitive contexts, precise Reported Speech protects the person you are reporting about. The tense shifts matter: choosing the wrong form can make something sound more serious, more current, or more permanent than it was.

“I have been feeling anxious.” → She said she had been feeling anxious.
“I haven't talked to anyone.” → She said she hadn't talked to anyone.
“I was afraid.” → She said she had been afraid. (or: she said she was afraid)
“Can the company help me?” → She asked if the company could help her.
Direct (sensitive context)Reported formWhy it matters
"I am struggling."She said she was struggling.Past form = something she said at a point in time, not necessarily now
"I have been feeling this way for months."She said she had been feeling that way.Past Perfect Continuous — duration in the past
"I don't know how to ask for help."She said she didn't know how to ask.Preserves her uncertainty without exaggerating it
"Will the company support me?"She asked if the company would support her.will → would; genuine question, not accusation
"I want to normalize this."She said she wanted to normalize it.want → wanted; her intent at that moment
Precision errors that change meaning:
✗ "She said she IS struggling." → She said she was struggling. (Using present tense makes it sound ongoing right now — that may not be accurate)
✗ "She asked if the company WILL support her." → She asked if the company would support her. (Using will sounds like a demand; would preserves question tone)
✗ "She said she NEVER talked to anyone." → She said she hadn't talked to anyone. ("Never" is an addition to what she actually said — this is misreporting)
🗣️ Micro-Speaking: Think of something sensitive a colleague or friend has shared with you about how they were feeling. Without using their name, report it to your partner in 2 sentences. Be precise. 45 seconds.
Teacher note — Grammar S4A: This is the most nuanced grammar session of the module. The meta-lesson is that Reported Speech is not just a grammar rule — it is an ethical tool. The wrong form can misrepresent someone. This connects grammar to professional behaviour and is something students remember long after the session. Write on the board: "What you report is what you are responsible for." This is a real-world professional standard.

Error to watch: Students who keep present tense in reported speech ("she said she IS...") after sensitive disclosures — gently correct and explain why: "If you say IS, it sounds like she is still struggling right now. Is that what she said? Was that at a specific moment?" Context matters.

🎧 Listening — The HR Meeting

10–12 min
Listening · Rodrigo reports an HR meeting about mental health · Vertex Logistics, Celaya

Rodrigo Vega attended a company-wide HR meeting about the new mental health initiative at Vertex Logistics. He reports back to a team member who missed it. Listen carefully for precision — how he reports what was said and asked, and whether he stays accurate.

The Mental Health Initiative — Vertex Logistics, Celaya
B1.1 · M5 · S4A · Voice: Adam (American English) · Speed: 0.85 · ElevenLabs
So the HR director presented the new mental health initiative at Vertex yesterday. I want to tell you what was said because it directly affects our team. She opened by saying that the company had decided to take employee wellbeing more seriously. She said she knew that mental health had not always been a priority here, and she acknowledged that some people had been struggling in silence. She told us that from this month, every employee would have access to six free therapy sessions per year through an external provider. Then she talked about psychological safety. She said it was the foundation of a healthy team, and she asked us whether we felt safe to raise concerns in our own departments. There was silence in the room for a moment. She said she understood why that question was uncomfortable, and she told us the company wanted to change that. Someone in the room asked her what would happen if a person sought help and their manager found out. She said that all therapy sessions were completely confidential and that no manager would be informed. She told us that was non-negotiable. She also asked us to think about one person in our team who might be struggling and to check in with them this week. She said we did not need to ask directly about mental health — just ask how they were really doing and listen. Honestly, I thought it was one of the most honest HR meetings I have attended in years. She said the company had a long way to go, but she told us they were committed to the change. I believe her.
Listening note: This script contains multiple layers of Reported Speech: Rodrigo reporting the HR director's statements, questions she asked, and even a question from the room. Before the second listen, ask students: "How many different people's words is Rodrigo reporting?" Answer: 2 (the HR director and an anonymous room participant). This develops listener awareness of Reported Speech depth.

Has the conversation about mental health at your company or in your industry changed in the last 3–5 years? Report something that was said — by a manager, HR, or a colleague — that showed the shift. Use precise Reported Speech.
Grammar hint: "She said the company had decided to... / He told us they were going to... / They acknowledged that..."
S↔S Question 2 — The Check-In
Role-play: Partner A asks Partner B "How are you really doing?" Partner B responds honestly about something at work. Then A reports the conversation to the class: "My partner said they had been feeling... They also told me that... I asked them whether..."
Grammar hint: Use all three types — statement, question, and precise tense
S↔S Question 3 — The HR Proposal
Imagine you are proposing a mental health initiative to your company's leadership. Report what employees have said they need: "Several colleagues told me they wanted... One team member said she had been feeling... The team asked whether the company could..."
Grammar hint: Building a professional case using Reported Speech as evidence
Class Question 4 — Stigma in Your Sector
Is there still stigma around mental health in manufacturing, logistics, or corporate environments in Mexico? What would someone need to say — or ask — to start changing that? Use Reported Speech to frame what needs to change.
Vocabulary hint: stigma, normalize, psychological safety, acknowledge, destigmatize
Class Question 5 — One Person
Think of the HR director's challenge: "Think of one person in your team who might be struggling, and check in with them this week." Without naming anyone, could you do that? What would you say? How would you start? Practice the opening in English right now.
Colloquial hint: "How are you really doing?" — say it, mean it, then listen
⚡ Solo Challenge — 90 seconds

Report a real or professional conversation about mental health or wellbeing you have had or witnessed. Use: at least 2 reported statements, 1 reported question, and 1 precision choice (why you chose that exact tense). Connect naturally.

Teacher note — Q5 is the heart of the session: The HR director's challenge ("think of one person and check in") is reproduced here as a speaking task because it works. Students who actually articulate how they would start that conversation in English are doing something professionally and linguistically meaningful simultaneously. Do not rush this question.
Speaking layers — Q3 HR Proposal: This is the most sophisticated task in the module: using Reported Speech as professional evidence in a proposal. Layer 1 — Are they using Reported Speech at all? Layer 2 — Are they being precise about tense (had wanted vs. wants)? Layer 3 — Are they using connectors to build a case rather than just listing quotes? The third layer is B1.2-adjacent fluency. Celebrate it when it happens.
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work
3 Phrases for This Week
PHRASE 1
"How are you really doing?"
The most valuable phrase in this session. Use it once this week with someone you work with — and actually listen to the answer. Stress "really." In English, this is a signal that you are not just making small talk. It opens the door. You do not need to say anything else — just listen.
PHRASE 2
"She said the company had acknowledged the problem."
A precision Reported Speech sentence you can use to brief a colleague after any HR or management meeting. It shows you understood what was said AND can communicate it accurately. Replace "the problem" with whatever was discussed. Professional, clear, B1.1-level fluency.
PHRASE 3
"It's okay to ask for help."
Say this to yourself if you need it. Say it to a colleague if they need it. Say it in a team meeting if the culture needs it. In English, this phrase normalizes vulnerability without dramatizing it. Six words. Real impact. Use it.
