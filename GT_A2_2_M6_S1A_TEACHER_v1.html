§3
Colloquial — Networking Closers
4 real phrases to end a professional conversation gracefully · 5 min
⏱ 5 MIN
"That's a great opportunity."
Use when someone tells you about a project, position, or deal that sounds promising. Shows enthusiasm without commitment. Very common in trade fairs and business meetings.
"We're expanding to three new cities next year." — "That's a great opportunity. You should talk to our distribution team."
"Let's stay in touch."
The standard professional goodbye after a networking conversation. Always follow with a concrete action — "I'll send you a LinkedIn request" or "I'll reach out next week."
"It was great meeting you, Carlos. Let's stay in touch — I'll find you on LinkedIn."
"It was great meeting you."
The classic closer for any first meeting. Warm, professional, and universally understood. Use this EVERY TIME you end a conversation with someone new at a business event.
"I have to go — my next meeting starts in 5 minutes. It was great meeting you, Andrea."
"I'll reach out next week."
Commit to a specific follow-up action — this transforms a casual encounter into a real business connection. Always more effective than "I'll be in touch" (vague).
"I'll reach out next week with our proposal. Is email or WhatsApp better for you?"
⚡ Teacher Note — Networking Closer Drill
Say: "Click each phrase to hear it — repeat it with me." Then run a 90-second roleplay: students pair up, one is leaving a networking event, the other tries to end the conversation using ALL FOUR phrases in sequence. Anticipated error: "Is a great opportunity" → "THAT'S a great opportunity" — the demonstrative is not optional here; it is part of the fixed chunk. Cultural note: Mexican professionals often extend goodbyes ("bueno, ya me voy, fue un gusto...") — in English, the closer is faster and more final. The phrase lands, then you leave. Teach this register difference explicitly: "In English, the closer is the last thing — not the beginning of five more minutes." "It was great meeting you" is past tense (not "it IS great") — this trips up A2.2 students. Drill the contraction: "It was" /ɪt wəz/.
§4
Grammar — Combining A2.2 Structures
Problem → Tool · Integrating used to / must / should / might / comparatives · 12–15 min
⏱ 12–15 MIN
🚨 Real Work Situation
You are at a trade fair in Guadalajara. Someone asks you three questions in 60 seconds: "Where do you work? What did you do before? What are your plans for next year? Which product do you recommend?" You want to answer naturally — using everything you know. But in Spanish you switch tools automatically. In English, you freeze after the first sentence.
The problem: you know EACH structure separately. You used to + modal + comparative + future plan — but combining them in one conversation feels impossible. Today you learn how to connect them.
🔧 The Tool — Integration: One answer, multiple structures
The secret is sentence connectors — small words that link your A2.2 tools into natural speech:

PAST HABIT (M1): "I used to work in sales..." + CONTRAST: "...but now I'm responsible for operations."
OBLIGATION (M2): "We have to submit the report by Friday," + ADVICE (M3): "so you should prepare the data today."
POSSIBILITY (M4): "The client might ask about pricing," + COMPARISON (M5): "so we need to show them our option is more competitive than the others."

KEY CONNECTORS: but / so / and / because / however / which means that
ModuleStructureExample in networking contextKey connector
M1 — Used toused to + verbI used to work in customer service, but now I manage a team of 12.but / now
M2 — Must/Have tomust/have to + verbWe have to confirm the order by next Friday — it's a hard deadline.so / which means
M3 — Should/Couldshould/could + verbYou should prepare a short introduction — they might ask about your experience.and / because
M4 — May/Mightmay/might + verbThe meeting might be in Guadalajara — I'm waiting for confirmation.however / but
M5 — Comparativesmore/adj-er + thanOur service is more reliable than the current supplier, and it's just as affordable.and / but
⚠️ Integration Errors — what students do at A2.2:
✗ Using structures in isolation: "I used to work in sales. I should prepare. The option is cheaper." — No connectors, no flow.
✓ Connected: "I used to work in sales, but now I manage procurement. You should check the proposal, because our option is more competitive."
✗ "I might to attend" → ✓ I might attend — no "to" after modal verbs (all modals: no infinitive "to")
✗ "I'm used to work" → ✓ I used to work — "I'm used to" is a different structure (B1+). Raise awareness now.

Drag the parts into the correct order to form integrated A2.2 sentences.

⚡ Teacher Note — Grammar: Integration Focus
This is the conceptual heart of M6. The grammar table is a visual reminder, not new information. Students have seen all five rows before — the challenge is the connector column. In Activity A, the key differentiations: Q2 (has to vs should) — "hard deadline" signals external obligation, not advice. Q3 (should vs must) — advice context, not strong rule. Q4 (might vs will) — the word "depends" signals uncertainty = might. In Activity B, the reorder establishes the PATTERN of complex sentence building: anchor structure + connector + result/comparison. After Activity B, ask: "How many structures did that second sentence have?" (3: must not + might + should) — this is the goal of M6: 3 structures per turn. Layer 2: ask students to rewrite Sentence 2 with their own content from work.

S-S Question 3 — Integrate

Class Question 4 — Discussion
At a networking event, is it better to talk to many people briefly, or have one deep conversation? Use comparatives to argue your position: "It's more [adj] to..." "A longer conversation is better than..."
Grammar hint: Use "more valuable / more effective / more natural / better / worse" with than.

⚡ Teacher Note — Speaking Section
Questions 1–2: factual recall and personal production — low-pressure start. Question 3: integration challenge — the goal is 3 structures in one answer. Listen for connector use ("and / so / because") — this is the mark of an integrated response vs three disconnected sentences. Question 6 (the 90-second intro) is the standout activity: after each student finishes, ask the class "Which A2.2 structures did you hear?" — peer identification builds listening AND grammar awareness simultaneously. Correction protocol: never interrupt the 90-second intro. Write errors silently. Review after the class votes.
