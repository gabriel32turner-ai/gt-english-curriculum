§1 Warm-Up — Project Story Quick-Fire 5–8 min
⚡ TEACHER NOTE — S2A OPENER

Reference S1A directly: "Last session we introduced the three-tense structure. Today we go deeper into describing past projects — the Simple Past as the professional storytelling tool." This session focuses on irregular verbs in narrative context and question structures. High payoff for the Exit Project.

How it started
What we did
The outcome
What we learned
1.Think of a project you worked on in the last 3 months. What was it called or what was it about?
2.Who was involved? Use names or roles: "My supervisor, the quality team, two engineers..."
3.Did it go well? What happened at the end — was it successful, delayed, or cancelled?
4.Can you tell the project story in 3 sentences? Start: "Last [month/quarter] we worked on..."
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 seconds
Say this right now: "Last week I had a meeting, made a decision, and sent an email." Three irregular past verbs. You already know all three. That's a project story in one sentence.
💡 ENGAGEMENT NOTE

Q4 is the seed for the Exit Project. Listen for fluency blockers: students often stop at irregular verbs and switch to present tense. Note 2-3 high-frequency errors — "we make the decision" vs "we made the decision" — and save them for the §5 error correction activity.

§2 Speaking — Tell Me About That Project 8–10 min
⚡ TEACHER NOTE — STORYTELLING FRAME

Introduce the 4-part project story frame: Context → Actions → Outcome → Lesson. Write it on the board. Students will use this frame in the Exit Project. Every round in this section practices one part of the frame.

T-S
Context round. Teacher sets up: "Valeria Torres had a big project last quarter at Solaris Manufacturing. She's going to describe it."

Question to students: "What do you think the project was about? Where did it happen? Who was involved?" — predict in Simple Past.

"Last quarter, Valeria worked on a supplier audit project. It started in February and lasted six weeks. Three departments were involved: quality, logistics, and purchasing. The main goal was to reduce delivery delays." — Context: who, when, where, what.

S-S
Actions round. Partner A describes what their team did during a real project. Use at least 4 past verbs. Partner B listens and asks one follow-up question: "Who did you work with?" / "How long did it take?" / "What did you find?"

A: "We visited three supplier plants, reviewed their quality systems, and wrote a final report. We also held two meetings with the purchasing team and presented our findings to the director."
B: "How long did the whole process take?"
A: "It took about six weeks from start to finish."

Class
Outcome + Lesson round. Each student gives the class one sentence about the outcome of their project AND one sentence about what they learned. Must use Simple Past for both.
"The project was successful because… / The most important thing I learned was…"

"The project was very successful — we reduced delivery delays by 30%. The most important thing we learned was that communication between departments was the key factor. We didn't have a good communication system before, so we created one."

§4 Colloquial — How Professionals Really Talk About Projects 5 min
⚡ TEACHER NOTE — REGISTER GAP

Students at A2.1 tend to describe projects in very formal, textbook English. These 4 phrases close the register gap — they're what native and fluent speakers actually say in post-project conversations and status meetings. Drill each one with a substitution: "Replace 'project' with a real one from your workplace."

"We pulled it off."
Means: we succeeded despite difficulty. Use after describing a hard project with a good ending.
"The deadline was impossible, but we pulled it off."
"We ran into some issues."
A professional way to say: we had problems. Less direct than "we had problems" — more natural in meeting language.
"We ran into some issues with the supplier in week two."
"We got it done on time."
The most satisfying sentence in project work. Simple, clear, professional — "on time" carries the weight.
"It was stressful, but we got it done on time."
"There were some lessons learned."
A mature, diplomatic phrase for "we made mistakes." Used in project debriefs. Shows professional reflection.
"Overall it was good — but there were some lessons learned."
💰 RETENTION NOTE

Ask: "Which of these phrases can you use in your next project debrief or status meeting?" Have 2-3 students commit. Then ask: "Can you combine two of these phrases in one sentence?" — "We ran into some issues, but we pulled it off." That's native-level project English at A2.1.

§5 Grammar — Irregular Past Verbs in Project Stories 12–15 min
Real situation: You're describing your project to a new colleague. You want to say: "We thinked about the problem, we maked a plan, and we writed a report."

Your colleague understands — but it sounds unnatural and signals low confidence. The tool: irregular past verbs are the backbone of professional project narratives. Getting them right signals fluency.
Base formPast formProject example
havehad"We had three critical meetings."
makemade"We made the decision last Tuesday."
findfound"We found a better solution."
writewrote"I wrote the final report in two days."
givegave"She gave the presentation on Friday."
taketook"The process took six weeks."
sendsent"I sent the specs to the engineering team."

bringbrought"They brought the new contract."
beginbegan"The project began in January."
⚡ TEACHER NOTE — IRREGULAR VERB DRILL

After the table, do a rapid "teacher says / class responds" drill. Say the base form: "make" → class calls out: "made!". Speed matters — this builds automaticity. Then add context: "The team — make — a decision." → "The team made a decision." Do 10 reps in 90 seconds, then move to Activity A.

§6 Listening — Daniel's Project Debrief 8–10 min
⚡ TEACHER NOTE — PRE-LISTENING TASK

Before playing: "You're going to hear Daniel Ochoa — Valeria's manager — give a project debrief to the regional team. Listen for: (1) how many irregular past verbs he uses, and (2) the outcome of the project." Students tally on paper. Compare after. Expected count: 8–10 irregular past verbs.

Daniel's Debrief — Solaris Manufacturing, Celaya
A2.1 · M6 · S2A · Voice: Daniel (Mexican accent) · Speed: 0.82
Good afternoon, everyone. I want to give you a quick debrief on the Q1 supplier audit project. As you know, the project began in January and took about six weeks to complete. Valeria Torres led the team — she did an excellent job. They visited four supplier facilities, including two in Guanajuato and two in Jalisco. The team found several quality issues and wrote a detailed report for each facility. We sent the final report to all department heads at the end of February. Now, we did run into some challenges. Two suppliers didn't send their documentation on time, which caused a short delay in week three. But the team found a solution — they made direct phone calls to each supplier and resolved the issue within 48 hours. The outcome was very positive. We identified the main causes of delivery delays and gave specific recommendations to each supplier. As a result, delivery performance improved by 25% in March — our best month in two years. I want to thank the whole team. They worked long hours and brought a lot of dedication to this project. The lessons learned from this audit are going to help us improve our supplier management process going forward. Any questions about the project? I'm happy to share more details.

COMPREHENSION — Multiple Choice

1. How long did the supplier audit project take?
2. What caused the delay in week three?
3. How did the team solve the delay problem?
4. What was the result in March?

🗣️ Quick: Summarize Daniel's debrief in 2 sentences. Use Simple Past. Then add one thing you would have done differently — use "I would..." or just "I think the team should have..." (only if students ask — don't teach this structure, it's B1+).

§SP Speaking Practice — Your Project Debrief 10–12 min
⚡ TEACHER NOTE — EXIT PROJECT SEED

This is the dry run for the Exit Project. Push students to use the full 4-part frame. When they finish a round, ask: "Can you make that more specific? Instead of 'we had problems' — what exactly happened?" Specificity is the A2.1 → B1 bridge. Take notes on 2-3 strong sentences you hear — read them back to the class at wrap-up as positive examples.

QUESTION 1 · T-S
Give me the CONTEXT of a real project. When did it start? Who was involved? What was the objective?
Use: last [month/quarter] / began / involved / The objective was to...
QUESTION 2 · T-S
Tell me the ACTIONS. What did the team actually do? Give at least 3 irregular past verbs.
Use: had / made / found / wrote / gave / sent / took / brought
QUESTION 3 · S-S
Tell your partner about a CHALLENGE during a project. What went wrong? How did you solve it?
Use: "We ran into..." / "The problem was..." / "We found a solution..."
QUESTION 4 · S-S
Give the OUTCOME and LESSON. Was it successful? What would you do differently? (Use simple language.)
Use: "In the end..." / "The result was..." / "We learned that..."
QUESTION 5 · Class
Full run: Give your complete 4-part project story to the class in under 90 seconds. Context → Actions → Challenge → Outcome.
Challenge: No stopping. No translating. All Simple Past. Go.
🎯 3 Phrases for This Week Use in your next project conversation
"We ran into some issues."
Replace "we had problems" with this in your next status meeting or email update. Sounds professional and natural.
"The process took [time]."
The simplest way to describe project duration. "The process took six weeks / three days / two months." Use it every time you describe a past project.
"We pulled it off."
When a project is hard but you succeed — this is what you say. Use it in a team email or post-project debrief. Your team will notice.
