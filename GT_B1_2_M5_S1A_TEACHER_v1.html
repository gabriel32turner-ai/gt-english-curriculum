§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

Should Influencers Be Regulated?

Q1  Personal
Do you follow any influencers online? What kind of content do they make? Why do you follow them?
Q2  Opinion
If an influencer promoted a product that turned out to be dangerous or fake — who must be responsible: the influencer, the brand, the platform, or the government?
Q3  Work
Could your company use an influencer to promote its products or services? Who would it be, and what might the risks be?
Q4  GT Challenge
Name one influencer you think is genuinely trustworthy and explain why. What makes them different from one who is just clout-chasing?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Complete this sentence out loud: “This influencer must be making a lot of money because…” — You just used modal deduction. That’s today’s grammar.
Q1 GT Original: ask each student to name one influencer they actually follow before starting. This creates immediate personal stakes. Some will feel embarrassed — that tension is productive. Q4 uses the M5 colloquial phrase “clout-chasing” before it’s formally taught — natural preview. Quick Win front-loads modal deduction naturally without grammar explanation.
Corporate learners (engineers, coordinators) may initially feel this topic is “not relevant to work.” Bridge it early: influencer marketing affects supply chains, brand perception, and HR. By end of session, they will see the professional relevance. Plant it in Q3.
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“If an influencer with 5 million followers promotes a fake investment and their audience loses money — who must be responsible?”

There are currently over 50 million people who identify as content creators globally. Influencers sell everything: cosmetics, crypto, car insurance, mental health apps, supplements, political ideas. Governments and platforms are beginning to regulate this space — but enforcement is inconsistent, penalties are rare, and millions of followers may still be misled before any action is taken. The question is no longer theoretical.

This session connects directly to the grammar: when we reason about responsibility without full information (“who must be responsible?”), we use modal deduction. Point this out explicitly after 90 seconds of pair discussion. “Notice how you said ‘they must know’ or ‘they can’t be unaware’ — that’s exactly what we’re learning today.”

§4   Colloquial  ·  8 min

Real Talk — M5 Phrases

“That’s so extra.”
Overdramatic, over the top, or unnecessarily theatrical
“She cried on camera about running out of filters. That’s so extra.”
“They’re clout-chasing.”
Doing something purely to gain fame, followers, or online attention — not for genuine reasons
“He posted that controversial opinion just to get engagement. Total clout-chasing.”
“It went viral.”
Spread rapidly and massively across social media, often reaching millions in hours
“The video of him apologising went viral — it must have hit 20 million views overnight.”
“Shade was thrown.”
Indirect, subtle criticism or disrespect aimed at someone, often without naming them directly
“She said ‘some people buy followers’ — shade was definitely thrown.”
“No cap.”
No lie, I’m being completely serious about this
“That documentary about fake influencers was the most shocking thing I’ve ever seen. No cap.”
These phrases come from online culture and may be new to some B1.2 corporate learners. They are authentic, current, and increasingly common in global business English (especially marketing, PR, and communications teams). Model them yourself — say “no cap” with confidence. Require learners to use at least one in §SP.
§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Modal Deduction — Reasoning About What You Don’t Know

⚠ REAL SITUATION
You’re watching an influencer promote a health supplement. She shows before/after photos, claims to have lost 15kg, and says “this changed my life.” There’s no #ad tag anywhere.

You want to reason out loud about what’s really happening. You need modal deduction: must / can’t / might.
✎ THE TOOL — MODAL DEDUCTION
modal + base verb  →  reasoning about present / past situations
ModalCertaintyInfluencer example
mustAlmost certain (positive)She must be sponsored — nobody recommends the same brand 10 times for free.
can’tAlmost certain (negative)She can’t have lost 15kg in 2 weeks — that’s medically impossible.
might / couldPossible but unsureShe might be telling the truth — or the results could be edited.
must havePast deduction (positive)She must have earned thousands from that post — the brand is huge.
can’t havePast deduction (negative)They can’t have known the product was dangerous when they promoted it.
⚠ NEVER: “She must to be” or “He can’t to know” — modal verbs NEVER take ‘to’.

Quick decision guide:

  • I’m almost sure it’s true → must be
  • I’m almost sure it’s NOT true → can’t be
  • It’s possible but I’m not sure → might be / could be
  • Past: almost sure it happened → must have + past participle
  • Past: almost sure it didn’t happen → can’t have + past participle

§6   Listening  ·  10–12 min

Carlos Mendez on the Attention Economy

OPINION METER — Before you listen:
“Influencers must be regulated by governments — self-regulation has clearly failed.”
👤 Strongly Disagree👥👥 Strongly Agree
50%
Carlos Mendez — Executive Coach — B1.2 M5 S1A — ElevenLabs speed 0.85
0:001:50

Carlos Mendez:

I coach executives. And increasingly, the question I’m asked is not “how do we grow our business?” — it’s “Carlos, should we work with influencers?” And my answer is always: it depends on what you value.

Here’s what I know. An influencer with 5 million followers must have significant power over purchasing decisions — brands don’t pay millions for nothing. But they might not use that power responsibly. And that’s the problem.

I’ve seen companies partner with influencers who can’t have properly tested the product — because the posts went up three days after signing. That’s not influence. That’s exploitation disguised as recommendation.

Now, regulation. Governments say they want transparency. The EU has new disclosure rules. The FTC in the US requires “#ad” labelling. But enforcement? It’s inconsistent at best. Influencers know this — they must be calculating that the risk of getting caught is lower than the reward of the sponsorship.

My view: self-regulation has failed. Not because influencers are evil — most are just trying to build a business. But because the incentive structure rewards reach over responsibility. Until that changes, audiences will keep being misled.

§SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

Reason Out Loud

RECALL
What does Carlos mean by “the incentive structure rewards reach over responsibility”? Put it in your own words.
Grammar hint: Try “He must be saying that…” to paraphrase his argument
DEDUCTION
Think of a real influencer (famous or local). Make 3 deductions about their life using must / can’t / might.
E.g.: “They must have a team. They can’t sleep much. They might feel…”
WORK CONTEXT
Your company is considering hiring an influencer to promote its products. What must you check before signing any agreement? What risks might exist?
Grammar hint: Use “You must verify…, there might be…, and they can’t…”
OPINION
Should influencers be licensed like doctors or financial advisors — needing qualifications before giving advice? Argue your position.
Grammar hint: “They must be held accountable if…” / “That can’t work because…”
DEBATE
Someone says: “Followers are adults — they can decide for themselves.” Agree or disagree and use deduction language to support your position.
Use a M5 phrase: “No cap — that can’t be the whole story if…”
CHALLENGE
You have 60 seconds. Convince your group that one specific regulation (disclosure rules / platform bans / fines) would actually work. Use modal deduction to back up your argument.
Lead with your must/can’t deduction, then support with evidence or logic
Q2 (deductions about an influencer) is the highest-engagement activity in this section. Ask learners to choose their own influencer and make live deductions — this produces personal, authentic language. Q6 (convince the group) is the production peak: 60 seconds, specific regulation, modal deduction throughout. Time it. Do not correct during delivery. Collect 2-3 patterns for post-delivery feedback.
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“They must be doing something right.”
Use it when a colleague’s result is impressive but the method isn’t obvious. Sounds analytical and professional — not envious.
“That can’t be the whole story.”
Use it in meetings when data or a report seems incomplete. Signals critical thinking without direct accusation. Works with leadership.
“It went viral for a reason — and we should understand it.”
Use it in marketing, communications, or project discussions. Shows you think beyond the surface of trends and content performance.
