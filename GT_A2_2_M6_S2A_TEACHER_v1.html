§3
Colloquial — Career Talk Phrases
4 natural expressions for talking about your professional path · 5 min
⏱ 5 MIN
"I'm moving up."
Informal way to say you are advancing in your career. Sounds confident and natural at all professional levels.
"How is work? — Really well. I'm moving up — I might get the regional manager role next quarter."
"Back in the day…"
Casual opener for the past — equivalent to "antes" or "en aquel entonces." Natural partner for used to.
"Back in the day, I used to work 60-hour weeks. Now I am much better at managing my time."
"I'm looking forward to it."
Express enthusiasm about a future event. Warmer than "I am excited." Use after confirming a plan.
"We are going to launch the new product line in Q2. Honestly, I am really looking forward to it."
"It's a stepping stone."
Describes a current role as part of a bigger plan. Shows strategic thinking. Very well-received in interviews and networking.
"This coordinator role is not my end goal. For me, it's a stepping stone toward a director position in 3–5 years."
⚡ Teacher Note — Colloquial
Say: "Click each phrase to hear it — repeat with me." Then: "Use ALL FOUR in one 60-second career monologue." Model: "Back in the day, I used to work in purchasing. Now I am moving up — responsible for three departments. This role is a stepping stone to something bigger, and I am really looking forward to it." Key error: "I am looking forward" is always progressive — never simple present in casual speech. "Back in the day" is invariable — no plural. Cultural note: "stepping stone" lets Mexican professionals express ambition without sounding arrogant — teach this as a register tool.
§4
Grammar — Future in the Career Narrative
Problem → Tool · Going to / Will / Might · Certainty scale · 12–15 min
⏱ 12–15 MIN
🚨 Real Work Situation
You are at a networking event. Someone asks: "What are your plans for next year?" You want to say: (1) your company is DEFINITELY opening a new office in León — the board approved it last month, and (2) you MIGHT get promoted, but it depends on results. You know "might" from M4, but you forget which future form shows certainty vs possibility. So you say "I will open a new office" for everything — even when you mean "maybe."
The problem: "will," "going to," and "might" all talk about the future — but they signal very different levels of certainty. Using the wrong one sends the wrong professional signal.
🔧 The Tool — Three Futures, Three Certainty Levels
Think of a certainty scale:

100% certain + planned: be going to → "We are going to open a new office. The board approved it."
Spontaneous / certain this moment: will → "I'll send you the brochure right now."
30–60% — possible, not sure: might / may → "I might get promoted next quarter — it depends on results."

In a career narrative: going to = your plan. Might = your possibility. Will = spontaneous commitment. Never interchangeable in professional English.
StructureCertaintyWhen to useCareer example
be going to + verb~100%Confirmed plans — decision already madeWe are going to expand to three cities. / I am going to specialize in supply chain.
will + verb~100%Spontaneous decisions, promises, offers made in the momentI will send you my LinkedIn profile. / I will reach out next week.
might / may + verb30–60%Possibilities you are not sure about; depends on other factorsI might get promoted if we hit the targets. / The company may open a new branch.
Present continuous~100%Fixed, confirmed future arrangements (calendar events)I am meeting the director on Thursday. / We are flying to Monterrey on Friday.
⚠️ Common Errors — future in career narratives:
✗ "I will probably change area next year." → ✓ I might change area — "probably" + "will" is redundant; use might for uncertainty
✗ "I might probably get promoted." → ✓ I might get promoted — never stack two uncertainty markers
✗ "We going to expand." → ✓ We are going to expand — "be" is obligatory; never drop it
✗ "I am going to specialize, maybe." → ✓ I might specialize — going to = confirmed plan, not possibility
⚡ ACTIVITY A — Which future form fits?

Read the context clue in brackets. Choose the correct future form.

1. "_____ send you the product catalogue right now. Do you have your email handy?" [spontaneous decision, made in this moment]
2. "We _____ open a distribution center in Salamanca next year. The board approved it last month." [confirmed plan]
3. "I _____ get the regional manager position — but it depends on this quarter's review." [uncertain possibility]
4. "The director _____ attend the conference in Guadalajara. Her assistant confirmed it this morning." [confirmed arrangement]
5. "I _____ specialize in digital supply chain management. I start my certification in September." [personal plan, already decided]
🗣️
Micro-Speaking: Tell your partner TWO things about your professional future: one confirmed plan (going to) and one possibility (might). Use your real career. Example: "I am going to finish my MBA in December. I might apply for a senior position next year."
⚡ ACTIVITY B — Reorder the career story

Drag the parts into the correct order to form a natural professional introduction using all three time frames.

Sentence 1: (past → present → future possibility)

  • ⋮and I might take on a director role in the next two years.
  • ⋮I used to work in procurement,
  • ⋮but now I manage operations for the whole region,

Sentence 2: (confirmed plan + possibility + advice)

  • ⋮so you should register early — the seats fill up fast.
  • ⋮The company is going to offer leadership training next month,
  • ⋮and I might recommend a few people from my team,
⚡ Teacher Note — Grammar
Draw the certainty scale on the board: 100% (going to) / 100% spontaneous (will) / 30–60% (might). The visual prevents the most common confusion. Activity A context clues: Q1 "right now" → will; Q2 "approved last month" → going to; Q3 "it depends" → might; Q4 "confirmed this morning" → present continuous. Teach explicitly: "Read the context before choosing the verb — the clue is always in the situation, not the grammar table." After Activity B: "Can you replace the content in Sentence 2 with something from YOUR company?" forces immediate transfer to real context.

Andrea's Career Story — Vertex Logistics, Celaya
A2.2 · M6 · S2A · Voice: Sarah (American English) · Speed: 0.82 · ~55 sec
Hi, I'm Andrea Soto. Let me tell you a little about my career trajectory. Back in the day, I used to work in customer service for a small company in Irapuato. It was a good job, but I didn't use to have much responsibility. I decided to transition into logistics, and honestly, it was the best professional decision I have ever made. Now I am responsible for regional sales at Vertex Logistics in Celaya. My team covers the whole Bajío region — Guanajuato, Querétaro, and Jalisco. It is a bigger challenge than I expected, but I am looking forward to every part of it. As for the future, the company is going to expand to three new cities next year — that is already confirmed. I might also take on a director role if the expansion goes well. And personally, I am going to specialize in supply chain management. I start a certification program in September. Back in the day, I never thought this was possible. But these days, I can see a clear path ahead. This role is definitely a stepping stone.

S-S Question 2 — Apply
Tell your partner YOUR OWN career story using all three time frames. Use used to for the past, responsible for for now, and either going to or might for the future. Minimum 4 sentences.
Grammar hint: "I used to [past role]. Now I am responsible for [area]. I am going to [confirmed plan] and I might [possibility]."
S-S Question 3 — Integrate
Your partner describes a future plan at their company. You give advice using should and could. Then switch. Example: "We are going to expand to León." → "You should prepare a market study. You could also visit the area first."
Grammar hint: Combine M3 (advice) with M4 (future plan) — the natural combination in professional conversation.
Class Question 4 — Discussion
Class debate: Is it better to have ONE clear career plan (going to) or stay flexible (might)? Use both structures to defend your position. "I think you should have a clear plan because..." vs "I think you should stay flexible because..."
Grammar hint: "If you have a plan, you should... however, you might also miss..." — use M3 + M4 + comparatives from M5.

⚡ Teacher Note — Speaking
Q2 is the core production task — monitor for time frame mixing. Students who jump from past to future without a clear present are the ones who need most support. The "responsible for" phrase anchors the present. Q3 is the critical integration moment: M3 (should/could) + M4 (going to/might) in a natural turn. Listen for the connector "so you should" — more natural than "I think should." Q6 is the cumulative assessment — the 2-minute introduction should use at least 5 different A2.2 structures. Opinion Meter: planning vs flexibility debate maps directly onto going to vs might — grammar embedded in a real opinion.
🎯 This week, use these 3 phrases at work:
Practical English you can use starting tomorrow — no preparation needed.
"We are going to [confirmed plan]. It's already decided."
Use in any meeting or email to announce a confirmed future action. Replaces "we will" when the decision has already been made. Sounds more professional and certain in any business context.
"Back in the day, I used to [old habit]. Now I [current situation]."
Your career arc in one sentence. Use it the next time someone asks about your experience. Connects naturally to any professional introduction at a meeting, event, or interview.
"I might [possibility] — it depends on [condition]."
Use when you are not 100% sure about a future plan. More professional than "I don't know." Useful in planning meetings, project updates, and performance reviews when outcomes are uncertain.

⏳ The Three-Time-Frame Arc

A complete professional introduction has three parts: past (used to), present (responsible for / currently), and future (going to / might). Today you built all three in one connected story.

🔬 Certainty Scale

Going to = confirmed plan (~100%). Will = spontaneous decision. Might/May = possibility (30–60%). Using the right one sends the right professional signal. Never use them interchangeably.

🌟 Career Collocations

Four new phrases for immediate use: "Back in the day," "I am moving up," "It's a stepping stone," and "I am looking forward to it." These make your English sound natural and confident.
