🔥 Warm-Up — Gender Roles in Your World

⏱ 8 min
Speaking first · Personal stories · Real context
Q1  T-S
When you were a child, what were you told a “man” or “woman” should do? Have those ideas changed for you personally?
Q2  S-S
At your workplace, are certain roles still dominated by one gender? Is that a problem or just reality?
Q3  Class
Which gender stereotype annoys you the most — and why has it lasted so long?
Q4  T-S
If you had a daughter, what would you tell her about careers, ambition, and what society expects from women?
⚡ Quick Win — 20 seconds
Say this out loud right now: “If stereotypes were less strict, everyone would feel freer at work.”
That's Second Conditional in action. You already have the grammar — let's build on it today.
Frame Q2 carefully: “This isn't about blame — it's about noticing what already exists.” This prevents defensive reactions and opens up genuine observation. Q4 is intentionally about daughters (not sons) — notice who answers first. GT Original: “S2 gender roles is the most debate-friendly session of M4. Create two sides and let students switch positions halfway through.”

🗣 Speaking Topic — “Men don't cry?”

⏱ 10 min
Rodrigo Vega — Senior Analyst, Celaya plant
When his son was born, Rodrigo asked for 2 weeks of paternity leave. His manager said: “Men don't need that much time. You're not the mother.” Rodrigo felt angry but stayed quiet. Now he wonders: if more men challenged those expectations, would the workplace be fairer for everyone?
Round 1  ·  60s  T-S
“If you were Rodrigo, what would you say to his manager? Be specific — use Second Conditional.”
Round 2  ·  90s  S-S
“Ask your partner: Have you ever witnessed a gender stereotype at work? What happened, and what would you do differently now?”
Round 3  ·  90s  Class
“If companies truly valued equality, what would change? Name 3 specific things that don't exist yet.”
Model answer (Second Conditional + colloquial):
If I were Rodrigo, I'd be furious. That's messed up — paternity leave is not about being a mother, it's about being a parent. If companies offered equal leave, stereotypes would disappear faster. At the end of the day, it's about respect, not tradition. I'd probably ask for a meeting with HR — it's a red flag when a manager says something like that openly.
■ Grammar: Second Conditional x4 ■ Vocab: stereotype, equality, bias ■ Colloquial: that's messed up / at the end of the day / it's a red flag
GT Originals note: “This is the most debate-friendly session of M4. Create two sides and let students switch positions halfway through.” In Round 3: push beyond generic answers (“equal pay”) toward specific observable changes: “What would the CEO say differently? What would the job ads look like? What meetings would change?” Error to correct: “If I would be Rodrigo” → “If I were Rodrigo.”

📚 Vocabulary — Gender, Equality & Social Norms

⏱ 12 min
Nouns — 10 Core Words
gender role
/ˈdʒendər roʊl/
Social expectations about how men or women should behave
If gender roles were less strict, families would share childcare equally.
stereotype
/ˈsteriətaɪp/
An oversimplified, fixed idea about a group of people
Stereotypes about women being less ambitious are harmful and false.
equality
/ɪˈkwɒlɪti/
Having equal rights, opportunities, and treatment for everyone
If equality were real in the workplace, the pay gap would not exist.
bias
/ˈbaɪəs/
An unfair preference for or against someone or something
Unconscious bias affects who gets promoted — often invisibly.
norm
/nɔːrm/
A standard or pattern of behavior expected in a society
If social norms changed, workplaces would adapt within a generation.
expectation
/ˌekspekˈteɪʃən/
A belief about what someone should do or be
Expectations placed on men and women start in childhood, not at work.
discrimination
/dɪˌskrɪmɪˈneɪʃən/
Unjust treatment based on gender, race, or other group
Discrimination in hiring is illegal — but it still happens subtly.
leadership
/ˈliːdərʃɪp/
The position or ability to lead a group or organization
If more women held leadership roles, the culture would shift.
identity
/aɪˈdentɪti/
Who you are — including gender, culture, and values
Your professional identity should not be limited by your gender.
progress
/ˈproʊɡrɛs/
Movement forward toward a better or more advanced situation
Progress has been made, but slowly — and unevenly.
Verbs & Expressions — 10 Core Words
challenge
/ˈtʃæləndʒ/
To question or dispute something you think is wrong or unfair
If more people challenged stereotypes openly, things would change faster.
enforce
/ɪnˈfɔːrs/
To make sure a rule or norm is followed or maintained
Companies often enforce gender norms without realizing it.
break stereotypes
/breɪk ˈsteriətaɪps/
To act in ways that contradict or disprove a fixed assumption
The more we break stereotypes, the freer the next generation will be.
assume
/əˈsuːm/
To believe something is true without proof or confirmation
We assume men are natural leaders — that assumption is wrong.
evolve
/ɪˈvɒlv/
To develop or change gradually over time
Gender roles evolve — but not fast enough for most people.
promote
/prəˈmoʊt/
To support or actively encourage something to happen
Companies need to promote equality in visible, measurable ways.
discriminate
/dɪˈskrɪmɪneɪt/
To treat someone unfairly because of a characteristic they have
If a company discriminates, it loses talent and trust.
define
/dɪˈfaɪn/
To describe the meaning or limits of something
Who gets to define what success looks like for men and women?
question
/ˈkwɛstʃən/
To express doubt about or challenge an accepted idea
We need to question the norms we inherited without choosing them.
expect
/ɪkˈspekt/
To consider something likely or to require it from someone
If we expect different things from men and women, equality is impossible.

Discrimination vs. bias: Discrimination = observable action (“She wasn't promoted because she's a woman”). Bias = the internal attitude (“I unconsciously assume men are better leaders”). Both matter — bias leads to discrimination. Push students toward “question” and “challenge” in sentences — these are B1.2 agency verbs. They signal independent thinking in speaking assessments.

💬 Colloquial — M4 Phrases in Context

⏱ 7 min
“That's a red flag”
Warning sign about someone's behavior or values
"Your manager says 'women don't belong in engineering'? That's a massive red flag."
“That's messed up”
Something deeply unfair or morally wrong
"She got less pay for the same role? That's messed up — full stop."
“I called it”
You predicted something correctly
"I told everyone this would happen. I called it six months ago."
“At the end of the day”
What ultimately matters most
"At the end of the day, the company loses when talent leaves because of bias."
“That hit different”
Something had an unexpected emotional impact
"Hearing Rodrigo's story hit different — I've been in that exact situation."
🗣
Micro-Speaking — 45 seconds
Use 2 of today's colloquial phrases in one connected story about a gender-related situation at work. Make it real — a company, a moment, an outcome.
“That hit different” is Gen-Z/millennial slang that has crossed into professional contexts. It means something resonated more deeply than expected. Students love this phrase — and it appears naturally in post-conversation reflection (“That example hit different for me because...”). Encourage its use in §SP today.

🎯 Grammar — Second Conditional (Applied)

⏱ 15 min
Second Conditional
Imaginary situations · What would happen if things were different now
Real situation first: Someone asks you: “If gender roles didn't exist, how would your team be different?” To answer with confidence and precision, you need the Second Conditional. You have it. Use it.
✅ Form
If + Past Simple,
would + base verb
“If the culture were fairer, there would be less turnover.”
🔑 Key Rule
NEVER “would” in the if-clause
“If I were in charge” (NOT “If I would be”)
❓ Question Form
What would you do
if + past simple?
“What would you change if you were the CEO?”
⚠️ Most common error at B1.2: “If I would be in charge…” → “If I were in charge…” — the if-clause always uses Past Simple, never “would.” Correct it warmly and consistently every time you hear it.

🎧 Listening — “If the rules were the same for everyone” (Rodrigo)

⏱ 10 min
Rodrigo Vega
Senior Analyst · Celaya Plant · B1.2 Character · M4
0:001:05
B1.2 · M4 · S2A · Voice: Rodrigo (Male, Neutral English) · Speed: 0.85 · ElevenLabs
I've been thinking about what happened last year. My son was born, and I asked for two weeks of paternity leave. My manager looked at me and said: 'Men don't need that much time. You're not the mother.' If I had responded right then, I would have said: 'That's exactly the problem with how we think about parenthood.' But I stayed quiet. I needed the job. The thing is: if those norms didn't exist, men would feel freer to be present parents. And if men were more present at home, women would have more space to advance at work. It's connected. Stereotypes don't only hurt one gender. If companies questioned their assumptions about roles, the whole culture would shift. I'm not saying it's easy. I'm saying it's worth the conversation. At the end of the day, my son needed me. That should be enough.
Comprehension Questions
What did Rodrigo's manager say about paternity leave?
Why did Rodrigo stay quiet?
According to Rodrigo, who do stereotypes hurt?
What does Rodrigo think companies should do?
True / False
“Rodrigo spoke up immediately when his manager said that.”
“Rodrigo believes gender stereotypes affect both men and women.”
“Rodrigo thinks changing workplace norms would be too difficult.”
After comprehension, ask: “Which line from Rodrigo's story hit different for you?” Expect this to generate the most authentic speaking of the session. The line “Stereotypes don't only hurt one gender” is particularly powerful — male students often respond to this more openly than expected. This transitions naturally into §SP.

This Week — 3 Phrases You Can Use at Work

⏱ 3 min
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“If I were in your position, I would...”
When a colleague asks for advice on a difficult situation. It shows empathy and positions your suggestion as hypothetical, not bossy.
“That's a bit of a double standard, isn't it?”
When you notice something is applied differently to different people or genders. Professional, confident, and hard to argue with.
“At the end of the day, what matters is the result.”
When a conversation about gender, roles, or expectations gets stuck. Refocuses on outcomes over identity. Works in any meeting.
Ask each student: “Where specifically this week would you use phrase #2?” If they can't name a situation, the language hasn't landed. The “double standard” phrase is particularly powerful — students rarely know this expression and it works in professional, adult English at exactly the right level of directness.
