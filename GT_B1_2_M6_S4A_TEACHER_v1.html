§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

Remote Work vs Office — What’s Genuinely Better for You?

Q1  GT Original
First: what does your company think about remote work? Then: what do YOU think? Are those two answers the same — or is there a gap? That gap is what this session is about.
Q2  Honest
Where do you actually do your best work? Not where you’re expected to work — where do you personally produce the most, think the clearest, and feel most motivated?
Q3  Opinion
Although remote work has many advantages, what do you genuinely miss when you’re not in the office? Be specific — not “collaboration” in the abstract, but something concrete you lose.
Q4  Debate
Post-pandemic, many Mexican companies moved back to full office. Despite the evidence that hybrid models increase productivity, why do you think so many managers still prefer everyone in the building?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Complete this sentence out loud: “Whereas my company prefers [X], I personally thrive when [Y]. As a result, I…” — Two linking words, one honest truth about your work life.
GT Original for S4A: the gap between company preference and personal preference is the session’s engine. Most corporate learners in the Bajío region work in manufacturing or logistics environments where full remote is impossible or uncommon — so this topic has a specific local flavour. Q4 (why managers prefer office) produces the most honest discussion of power, trust, and control that M6 generates. Let it run if energy is high — the vocabulary (boundary, flexibility, isolation, culture) appears naturally in these answers.
S4A is the most immediately relevant session to learners’ current working lives. Unlike university or failure, which require memory and reflection, remote work vs office is happening now. Use that immediacy: “This isn’t a hypothetical debate — this is your life, today. How are you navigating it?”
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“Whereas the office was once the default and working from home was the exception, today it’s becoming a negotiation between what companies want and what individuals need. Who should win that negotiation — and who usually does?”

Since 2020, the debate over remote work has polarised organisations globally. Although productivity research consistently shows that hybrid arrangements benefit most knowledge workers, many senior leaders continue to push for full office return. As a result, thousands of professionals face a choice between their preferred work style and their job security. Furthermore, the debate has exposed something deeper: how much trust exists between organisations and the people who work for them. Despite the data, the decision is rarely made on evidence alone.

The speaking topic models all four linking word families again: whereas, although, as a result, furthermore, despite. After discussion, count them with the class and ask: “Which linking word here changes the direction of the argument most sharply?” Answer: “Despite the data, the decision is rarely made on evidence alone.” The contrast between data and reality is a recurring theme in B1.2 professional conversations.

§4   Colloquial  ·  5 min

Module 6 Phrases — In Work Preference Context

“I’m figuring it out.”
In the process of finding what works
“Whereas before I thought the office was always better, now I’m not so sure — I’m still figuring it out.”
“It’s a game-changer.”
Something that transforms a situation
“Although I was sceptical, working from home two days a week has been a game-changer for my focus.”
“That’s the goal.”
The clear objective you’re working towards
“A hybrid arrangement where I can manage my schedule freely? As a result of this experience, that’s the goal.”

Committed; no regret about the direction

§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Linking Words — Applied to Debate & Argument

⚠ REAL SITUATION
Your manager has announced that the team must return to the office five days a week starting next month. You disagree. You want to make a professional, structured case for a hybrid arrangement. You can’t just say “I prefer home” — you need to connect evidence, contrast expectations, and show the consequences. That’s what linking words do in professional debate.
✎ LINKING WORDS IN PROFESSIONAL DEBATE — BUILDING AN ARGUMENT
A strong argument needs contrast, evidence, consequences, and concessions. These linking words provide the architecture.
What you’re doingUseWork context example
Acknowledging the other sideAlthough / Even thoughAlthough I understand the benefits of the office, the productivity data shows otherwise.
Accepting difficulty, then pivotingDespite + -ingDespite working from home for two years, my output increased by 30%.
Showing logical consequenceAs a result / ThereforeThe commute adds 3 hours to my day. As a result, I am exhausted by Thursday.
Adding a supporting argumentFurthermore / In additionFurthermore, hybrid arrangements reduce absenteeism according to research.
Contrasting two realitiesWhereas / HoweverWhereas managers value visibility, employees value autonomy.
⚠ Remind: “Despite I prefer home” → WRONG. “Despite preferring home” or “Despite my preference for home” → CORRECT.

The parallel structure signals good English argument style. Carlos Mendez on Work Preference & Trust

OPINION METER — Before you listen:
“Despite the productivity evidence, the remote work debate is really about trust — and most managers don’t trust their teams as much as they claim to.”

Carlos Mendez — B1.2 M6 S4A — ElevenLabs speed 0.85

Carlos Mendez:

I have a simple test I use with leadership teams when they debate return-to-office policies. I ask: “If you had a team of 10 people and you couldn’t see what they were doing — ever — would you trust them to deliver?”

Although most leaders say “yes” immediately, their return-to-office policy says something different. They want people visible. They want proof of presence. And that tells me something very important about the relationship between managers and the people they lead.

Whereas productivity research consistently shows that knowledge workers perform as well or better remotely, the instinct to have people in a room persists. Furthermore, I think this is partly cultural in Mexico and Latin America — presence has historically been confused with commitment. If you’re in the building, you must be working. If you’re not, you might be doing something else.

Despite this, the shift is happening. Despite it being slow. As a result, the professionals who will thrive are those who can make their work visible without being physically present — those who can communicate their value clearly, in English and in every other language their organisation speaks.

§SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

Your Work Preference Argument

RECALL
What does Carlos mean by “presence has historically been confused with commitment?” Do you agree? Use whereas to structure your answer: whereas before / now.
Start: “Whereas before, being in the office meant… now, however…”
PERSONAL
Describe your ideal work arrangement honestly — not what’s available, but what would make you most productive, most fulfilled, and most connected to your team. Use linking words to justify each element.
although / despite / furthermore / as a result — one per claim
PROFESSIONAL
You need to make a case to your manager for a hybrid arrangement. Build the argument in 90 seconds using the debate structure from §5: although → despite → as a result → furthermore.
Partner plays the manager. They ask one follow-up: “But what about collaboration?” Answer in 20 seconds.
DEBATE
Argue the opposite of your actual preference. If you prefer remote, argue for the office — and vice versa. Use linking words to make the argument convincing even if you don’t believe it.
This is steelmanning — finding the best version of an argument you disagree with. B1.2 professional skill.
WORK
In your specific company and role, what is genuinely impossible to do remotely — and what is unnecessarily done in person? Where does the company lose productivity by insisting on office?
Use vocabulary: collaboration, productivity, culture, boundary, commute
CHALLENGE
Carlos says the shift is happening, even if slowly. What does your company need to change in terms of culture and trust before it can genuinely offer flexibility? And what’s your role in making that change?
Ends the session with forward-looking language — use “that’s the goal” or “no looking back” to close
Q4 (steelmanning) is the most analytically demanding task in S4A §SP. Requiring learners to argue convincingly for a position they disagree with produces the most sophisticated linking word use — because they need structure to compensate for lack of genuine conviction. Q6 (change in company) seeds the S5 Capstone naturally: where do you see yourself in 5 years? The answer begins with the changes you want to make now. Signal this: “Start thinking about this — next session is your Capstone.”
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“Whereas the team prefers flexibility, the policy requires presence.”
Use in any meeting where team preferences and company policy are in tension. Names the gap without assigning blame. Works in HR conversations, team reviews, and negotiations.
“Despite working remotely, our productivity has increased.”
One of the most useful sentences a professional can produce in an English-language performance discussion. “Despite” signals honesty; the rest states the evidence. Combine these two elements and you sound credible.
“Furthermore, this arrangement gives us better access to talent.”
Use when building a business case for remote or hybrid work. “Furthermore” signals a structured argument. The specific claim about talent access is hard to argue against.
