§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

Celebrity Culture — Inspiration or Distraction?

Q1  Personal
Name one celebrity you genuinely admire — not because they’re famous, but because of something specific they’ve done or said. Why them?
Q2  Opinion
Why do we care about people we’ve never met — people who don’t know we exist? What must be happening psychologically when millions follow a celebrity’s every move?
Q3  Work
Some companies use celebrities as brand ambassadors. Can you think of an example — local or global — where this worked brilliantly? Where it failed spectacularly?
Q4  Ethics
Paparazzi follow celebrities everywhere. Celebrity children are photographed without consent. At what point does public interest become an invasion of privacy?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Think of any celebrity scandal. Say out loud: “They must have known the risk. Their team can’t have been unaware. The public might not care in a year.” — Three perfect modal deductions in one sentence.
Q1 is key: “not because they’re famous, but because of something specific.” This filter forces learners past surface fandom into actual argument. Corporate learners often admire sports figures (Rafa Martínez, Checo Pérez) or business figures (Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos — with ambivalence). Q3 is the professional bridge — use brand ambassador failures as entry points: celebrity divorces, scandals, and how they affect endorsement contracts produces rich business vocabulary. Q4 seeds the paparazzi vocabulary naturally.
This session has the highest personal engagement risk of M5: learners may feel embarrassed to admit they follow celebrities, or may feel it’s “too trivial” for a corporate English class. Frame it early: “Celebrity culture is worth understanding professionally — it shapes brand perception, marketing strategy, and what millions of your customers value.” This converts personal topic into professional relevance.
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“The average person spends 2 hours a day consuming celebrity content. That can’t be without consequence — but is the consequence positive or negative?”

Celebrities are not just entertainers anymore. They are brands, political voices, mental health advocates, and cultural symbols. When Rihanna launches a beauty line, or a footballer speaks on climate change, or an actor reveals a personal struggle — millions listen. Celebrity culture can inspire: it surfaces causes, role models, and possibilities. But it can also distract, mislead, and make ordinary people feel their own lives are inadequate.

The topic statement itself uses “can’t be” as a modal deduction, modelling the grammar naturally. After 90 seconds of discussion, highlight this: “Notice I used ‘can’t be without consequence’ — that’s confident deduction. That’s what we’re practising today.”

§4   Colloquial  ·  5 min

M5 Phrases — In Celebrity Culture Context

“That’s so extra.”
Overdramatic, over the top
“She wore a 10-metre train to a fast food restaurant for a photoshoot. That’s so extra.”
“They’re clout-chasing.”
Seeking fame or attention cynically
“He posted that emotional video right before his album dropped. Classic clout-chasing.”
“Shade was thrown.”
Subtle public disrespect
“She said ‘I support all artists who actually write their own music.’ Shade was definitely thrown.”
“No cap.”
Completely serious, no exaggeration
“That celebrity interview must be the most uncomfortable thing I’ve ever watched. No cap.”
By S4A, these phrases should be becoming automatic for learners. Test retention: close the file and ask “What are the 4 M5 colloquial phrases?” before opening the screen. Any learner who can produce all 4 unprompted has genuinely internalised them — acknowledge that publicly.
§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Modal Deduction — Full Integration

⚠ REAL SITUATION
A famous footballer is seen leaving a hospital late at night. No official statement. His club says nothing. Gossip pages claim different things. You want to reason about what’s happening without making definitive claims. This is modal deduction in real professional communication.
✎ FULL MODAL DEDUCTION SYSTEM — INTEGRATION CHECK
TimeCertaintyStructureCelebrity example
PresentAlmost certain ✓must beHe must be going through something serious — he cancelled three events.
PresentAlmost certain ✗can’t beHis team can’t be unaware — they manage his every public move.
PresentPossiblemight/could beThe scandal might be exaggerated — the source is a gossip site.
PastAlmost certain ✓must haveSomeone must have leaked the photos — they were private.
PastAlmost certain ✗can’t haveShe can’t have known about the endorsement contract when she spoke out.
★ At B1.2: You should be able to move fluidly between all 5 forms in a single connected argument. That’s what makes you sound analytical in English.

Activity A Q1 is the most nuanced: “might” is also defensible (“we’re not sure of her motives”) — accept it if learners justify the uncertainty. The key teaching point: “can’t” is more confident than “might.” If you say “can’t,” you’re saying you’re almost certain. If you say “might not,” you’re saying it’s possible but you’re unsure. Activity B Sentence 2 has an unusual word order — the object of “build” is split across clauses. Point this out as a natural English construction; formal structure would be different.
§6   Listening  ·  10–12 min

Carlos Mendez on Fame and the Mind

OPINION METER — Before you listen:
“Celebrity culture does more psychological damage than it provides inspiration — particularly to young people.”
👤 Strongly Disagree👥👥 Strongly Agree
50%
Carlos Mendez — B1.2 M5 S4A — ElevenLabs speed 0.85
0:001:50

Carlos Mendez:

In my coaching work, I ask executives one question that always generates discomfort: “Who do you compare yourself to?” Most say: peers, competitors, colleagues. But then I ask about social media. And there it is. They’re comparing their internal reality — their doubts, their failures, their ordinary Tuesday — to a celebrity’s curated public highlight reel.

That comparison must be damaging in ways people don’t consciously register. You’re not comparing like with like. You’re comparing your worst moments to someone else’s best performed ones.

Now — is celebrity culture all negative? No. I think it might be one of the most powerful vehicles for social change when used well. A celebrity speaking openly about mental health can reach millions who would never attend a workshop. An athlete endorsing a cause can’t be underestimated in its reach.

The problem can’t be celebrity culture itself. The problem is the absence of critical distance. We consume celebrity content without asking: whose interest does this serve? What am I being sold? And what am I comparing myself to?

If you can ask those three questions every time you open Instagram, you must be one of the most media-literate people in your organisation.

§SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

The Celebrity Analysis

RECALL
Carlos talks about comparing your “internal reality” to a “curated highlight reel.” Explain this concept in your own words using “must be” or “might be.”
Start: “He must be suggesting that…”
DEDUCTION
Think of one celebrity you’ve seen recently in the news. Apply Carlos’s three questions: whose interest does this serve? What are you being sold? What’s the comparison trap?
Use all three modals across your analysis: must / can’t / might
BRAND / WORK
Your company is considering partnering with a celebrity for a campaign. What must you check about their brand alignment? What scandals might kill the partnership?
Use vocabulary: brand, persona, image, endorse, scandal, idolize
DEBATE
Carlos says celebrities who speak openly about mental health can reach millions. But does celebrity endorsement trivialise serious issues? Argue both sides.
Use “shade was thrown” or “clout-chasing” if relevant to your argument
PERSONAL
Have you ever caught yourself comparing your life to someone famous? What must have triggered that comparison? Looking back, was it a fair comparison?
Use “I might have been…” for honest reflection — this is safe, not embarrassing
CHALLENGE
Design a “media-literate” corporate training programme based on Carlos’s three questions. What would each module teach? Who must attend?
Use must have / must be / might be across your proposal — this is real B1.2 professional production
Q6 (media literacy training programme) is the bridge between M5 content and the real professional world — it’s the most appropriate production challenge for corporate learners. Q3 (celebrity brand partnership) is the most immediately relevant to marketing, HR, and leadership learners. Q5 (personal reflection) is the most emotionally honest — model it yourself before asking. Remember: this session previews the S5 Pitch task — tell learners at the end of §SP: “Next session, you’ll need to think about a film or series you can pitch. Start thinking now.”
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“Whose interest does this serve?”
Use it in any meeting where a proposal or piece of information is presented. Signals strategic thinking and intellectual independence. Hard to argue with.
“Their brand can’t survive another scandal like that.”
Use “brand” for people and companies alike. Combines modal deduction with professional vocabulary. Works in competitive analysis, HR conversations, and supplier evaluations.
“The persona doesn’t match the product — that must be the disconnect.”
Use when analysing why a marketing campaign, a hire, or a partnership isn’t working. “Persona” and “must be” together sound sharp and analytical.
