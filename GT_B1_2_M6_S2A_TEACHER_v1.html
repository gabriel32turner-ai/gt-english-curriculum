§1   Warm-Up  ·  5–8 min

What Does Success Mean to You?

★ GT ORIGINAL — Before you start: 60 seconds of silence
Give each student a piece of paper or ask them to type in their phone. They have 60 seconds to write their personal definition of success. No talking. No comparing. Just their honest, private answer. Collect or keep it. You will return to it at the end of §8.
Q1  Personal
Name one person — real or fictional — that you personally consider successful. Not famous, not rich necessarily — just someone whose life represents success to you. Why?
Q2  Opinion
At what age did your definition of success change the most? What happened — and what changed? Although you might not have noticed it then, when did the shift happen?
Q3  Debate
Is financial success the same thing as personal success? Think of someone who is both very wealthy and clearly unhappy. What does that tell us?
Q4  Work
Could you honestly say your company’s definition of success matches yours? Where does it align — and where does it conflict? As a result of that gap, how do you feel on most Mondays?
⚡ QUICK WIN — 30 SECONDS
Complete this sentence out loud: “Whereas I used to define success as [X], now I define it as [Y]. As a result, I [Z].” — Three clauses, two linking words, one truth about your life.
GT Original: the 60-second writing exercise is the emotional anchor of S2A. Do it before ANY discussion. The silence is productive — don’t fill it. Keep the papers. At the end of §8, return them and ask: “Look at what you wrote an hour ago. How much of it do you still believe?” People are genuinely surprised. This is the most memorable moment of the session. Q4 (company vs personal success) is the most professionally charged — let learners be honest. If they say “I don’t think about Mondays”, that’s data too.
§2   Speaking Topic  ·  5 min

The Big Question

“Whereas previous generations measured success by what they owned, today’s professionals are increasingly measuring it by what they feel. Is that progress — or is it just a different kind of pressure?”

For most of the 20th century, success had a simple formula: a stable job, a house, a family, a car. Although these markers still matter, they are no longer sufficient for most educated professionals. Fulfillment, purpose, balance, and legacy have entered the conversation. As a result, the definition of success has expanded — but furthermore, the pressure to achieve it has expanded alongside it. We want more, feel more, and judge ourselves by more criteria than any previous generation. The question is whether that makes us more alive or simply more anxious.

The speaking topic deliberately uses all four linking word families. Count them with learners after discussion: “whereas,” “although,” “as a result,” “furthermore.” Ask: “Which linking word changed the direction of the argument most?” This is metalinguistic awareness at B1.2 level.

§4   Colloquial  ·  5 min

Module 6 Phrases — In Success Context

“That’s the goal.”
Your clear, stated objective
“Financial freedom by 50? Although that sounds far away, that’s the goal.”
“I’ve come a long way.”
Reflecting on genuine personal progress
“Whereas before I measured success by salary, now I measure it by fulfilment. I’ve come a long way.”
“No looking back.”
Committed to forward momentum, no regret
“Despite the uncertainty, I left the corporate job and started my own business. No looking back.”
“I’m figuring it out.”
In the process of finding your direction
“I don’t have all the answers about what success means to me yet — I’m still figuring it out.”
This session introduces “No looking back” for the first time. Ask learners if they have a decision in their career they genuinely don’t regret — then ask them to describe it using “despite + -ing” and “no looking back.” The emotional authenticity of the answer will naturally produce the grammar correctly.
§5   Grammar  ·  12–15 min

Linking Words — Applied to Personal Argument

⚠ REAL SITUATION
Your manager asks why you declined the promotion last year. You want to give a structured, honest answer that shows self-awareness — not just “I didn’t want it.” You need to connect your reasons, contrast your past and present thinking, and show the consequence. That’s exactly what linking words do.
✎ LINKING WORDS IN PERSONAL ARGUMENT — FOCUS SESSION
Today: using linking words with the vocabulary of success, values, and personal growth. Same rules, new context.
You want to…UseExample with S2A vocabulary
Show contrast between past + present youwhereas / althoughWhereas wealth was my priority at 25, balance matters more to me now.
Accept something negative, then redirectdespite + -ingDespite not getting the recognition I wanted, I stayed because the purpose was real.
Explain the consequence of a decisionas a result / thereforeI redefined my goals. As a result, I’m more motivated than I’ve ever been.
Build your argument with extra evidencefurthermore / in additionThe role offered better balance. Furthermore, it aligned with my long-term purpose.
Contrast two positions fairlyhowever / neverthelessThe salary was lower. However, the fulfillment more than compensated for it.
⚠ Reminder: “Despite she was ambitious” → WRONG. “Despite being ambitious” or “Despite her ambition” → CORRECT.

Carlos Mendez on Redefining Success

OPINION METER — Before you listen:
“Although wealth is a valid measure of success, it is not a sufficient one — and those who think otherwise have not yet experienced having it.”

0:001:50

Carlos Mendez:

I have coached people worth fifty million dollars who describe themselves as failures. I have coached people earning less than the average salary in Mexico who consider themselves among the most successful people they know. Although the gap between those two groups is enormous in financial terms, it is often almost invisible in terms of life satisfaction.

What I have learned is this: success is not a destination. It is a relationship between what you value and what you’re actually doing. Whereas most people spend the first half of their career accumulating — wealth, recognition, credentials — they spend the second half trying to figure out what those things are actually for.

The most dangerous word in the vocabulary of success is “when.” When I get the promotion. When I pay off the debt. When I reach the goal. Furthermore, the research on this is consistent: deferred fulfilment rarely arrives. As a result, the most successful people I know are those who have learned to find purpose in the journey — not just the destination.

Despite having everything they set out to achieve, many of my clients felt nothing when they got there. The goal had moved. It always does.

Carlos’s most quotable line — “The goal had moved. It always does.” — is the session’s emotional peak. After comprehension, write it on the board and ask: “Do you believe this? §SP   Speaking Practice  ·  10–12 min

Your Definition of Success

RECALL
Carlos says success is “a relationship between what you value and what you’re doing.” Using that definition, how successful are you right now? Be honest and use linking words to structure your answer.
Use: although / however / as a result — one in each clause
PERSONAL
Think of a moment when you achieved something and felt less happy than expected. What happened? Despite achieving the goal, what was missing?
Use: despite + -ing + a contrast word to explain the gap
DEBATE
Is it possible to be successful at work and unsuccessful in life — or vice versa? Build both sides of the argument using linking words to connect your evidence.
Whereas one definition says… Furthermore… However…
WORK
Your company is being evaluated on “success.” What metrics does it use — and which ones does it ignore? Although results matter, what else should be measured?
Build a case for non-financial success metrics using furthermore / in addition
CHALLENGE
Design your personal “success scorecard” — 5 criteria you would use to evaluate your own life at 60. Why those 5? Use linking words to justify each choice.
Be specific. Vague answers (“be happy”) don’t qualify — define measurable criteria
COLLOQUIAL
Compare where you were professionally 5 years ago to where you are now. Use at least 3 M6 phrases: “I’ve come a long way,” “that’s the goal,” and “no looking back.” Link them with discourse markers.
This should feel like a real TED talk opening — honest and structured
Q5 (success scorecard) is the session’s highest-value production task. It requires learners to define, justify, and connect five criteria — demanding vocabulary, linking words, and genuine personal reflection simultaneously. Q6 (TED talk opening) is the emotional reward for the session. Model it yourself briefly: “Five years ago I was… As a result of [decision], I’ve come a long way. That’s the goal.” Learners often produce their best English in this prompt because the content is authentically theirs.
🎯 This week, you can use these 3 phrases at work:
“Although the results were strong, we need to redefine what we’re measuring.”
Use in any performance review, KPI discussion, or strategy meeting. Shows you think beyond surface metrics. The phrase “redefine what we’re measuring” is powerful and rare in most corporate meetings.
“Furthermore, this gives the team a clear sense of purpose.”
Use when justifying a decision or proposal by adding an argument about motivation and engagement. “Furthermore” signals that you’re making a structured argument, not just listing.
“Whereas before this felt like a distraction, I see it as an opportunity now.”
Use when you’ve changed your mind on something at work. Shows intellectual flexibility and maturity. It’s the kind of sentence that signals a senior professional mindset.
