A2.1 · Module 5 · Session 3B — Application & Mastery
Will vs Going To:
The Complete Contrast
Pure production session — no new grammar. Students apply everything from S3A: choosing will vs going to based on context (spontaneous decision, offer, promise, opinion-prediction vs pre-plan, evidence-prediction). Every activity demands immediate spoken choice under time pressure.

§1
Hot Take — "Going To and Will Are Basically the Same."
Instant opinion · Use both forms under pressure · 60 seconds · b1 badge
⏱ 5 MIN
Context: Your colleague says: "I don't bother choosing between will and going to — I just pick one randomly. My clients never notice the difference." You disagree.
"Going to and will mean the same thing in business English — native speakers don't care which one you use. Agree or disagree — you have 60 seconds."

💡 Layer 1 — Accuracy: Listen for the contrast itself — did the student use both forms? The key error at A2.1: using "going to" for everything (treats it as the default) OR using "will" for pre-planned actions ("I will meet the client tomorrow" when it's already scheduled = should be "I'm going to meet"). Flag either error immediately.
🔥 Layer 3 — Authenticity: Ask: "Has a native English speaker or a client ever corrected your going to / will usage? Or have you ever hesitated not knowing which one to use?" Real experience anchors the grammar distinction emotionally.
§2
Contrast Debate — "Which Form Sounds More Professional?"
2 students · Each defends a position · Must use their assigned form in every argument
⏱ 10 MIN
Scenario: Two colleagues are preparing a client presentation. Side A argues that "will" sounds more decisive and professional for all future situations. Side B argues that "going to" signals preparedness and planning — what clients actually want to see. You are assigned one side. Defend it using your assigned form in every argument.
Side A — Will Is More Professional
"I'll lead the client meeting. We will definitely deliver the results. Everything will go smoothly — trust us."
  • "I'll take ownership of this project — doesn't that sound more confident than 'I'm going to take ownership'?"
  • "When a client asks for help, 'I'll fix it right now' is faster and more reassuring than 'I'm going to fix it.'"
  • "Will won't make a client wait — 'I'll send it today' closes the loop immediately."
  • "The word 'will' signals a promise. Going to sounds like you might change your mind."
Side B — Going To Shows Real Preparation
"We're going to launch on schedule. We've planned every phase. The client is going to see a team that's ready."
  • "If I say 'I'm going to present the strategy on Thursday,' the client knows it's already prepared."
  • "Going to tells the client there's a plan — not just a good intention."
  • "Isn't 'We're going to hit target' more credible than 'We'll hit target' — one has evidence, the other is just hope?"
  • "I'm going to call you back this afternoon shows I've already scheduled it. 'I'll call you' is vague."
🎭 Layer 2 — Engagement: Both sides are partially right — this debate has no winner, which is the point. After 3 minutes debrief: "Actually, both forms are correct in different contexts. The question is WHICH context." This reframes the debate result as the grammar insight. Students who argued passionately are now emotionally invested in understanding the rule.
💡 Pedagogical focus: The key insight: "Will = spontaneous / offers / promises / opinion-predictions." "Going to = pre-plans / evidence-predictions." Neither is universally more professional — context decides. This is the B-session's pedagogical core. Spend 3 minutes on this after the debate.

Relay Chain — "Q1 Planning Session"
Team relay · Alternate will and going to · Each card specifies the form · No repeats
⏱ 8 MIN
Situation: Your team is in the Q1 planning meeting. Each person contributes one sentence about the upcoming quarter. The card tells you which form to use — you choose the content. Make it realistic.
RELAY CARD 1 — SPONTANEOUS OFFER (WILL)
The manager just said there's no one to handle the client follow-ups. Volunteer right now using will.
Model: "I'll take care of the follow-ups. I'll send status updates every Monday."
RELAY CARD 2 — PRE-PLANNED ACTION (GOING TO)
Your team already scheduled the product launch for March. State the plan.
Model: "We're going to launch the new product in March — the campaign is ready and the team is briefed."
RELAY CARD 3 — OPINION PREDICTION (WILL)
No hard data — just your gut. Predict how Q1 revenue will go.
Model: "Revenue will probably increase if we close the two pending contracts. I think it'll be a strong quarter."
RELAY CARD 4 — EVIDENCE PREDICTION (GOING TO)
You have data: last quarter was 18% above target, demand is growing, two new clients just signed. Predict with evidence.
Model: "Look at these numbers — Q1 is going to break our record. The pipeline is the strongest it's ever been."
RELAY CARD 5 — PROMISE (WILL / WON'T)
The team is worried about the tight deadline. Make two commitments — one positive promise and one negative guarantee.
Model: "I'll personally monitor the deadline every week. We won't let the client down — that I can promise."
💡 Layer 1 — Form tracking: Write on the board two columns: WILL and GOING TO. As each student speaks, mark which form they used. The goal is not just accuracy on their assigned card — but also awareness that both columns fill naturally in a real business conversation. The relay is a mini-replica of how these forms coexist.
🎭 Layer 2 — Challenge mode: After the chain, ask: "Could we have used the opposite form for any of these cards? What would change?" Cards 3 and 4 are especially interesting: switching forms changes the implied confidence and evidence level.

📱 Your phone rings — it's a client you haven't spoken to in 6 months. You decide right now to set a follow-up meeting.
Model: "I'll schedule a catch-up call for next week. — WILL (spontaneous decision made at moment of speaking)"
📅 You booked a client dinner three weeks ago. In the morning you remind a colleague.
Model: "I'm going to have dinner with the Ruiz account tonight — it's been in the diary since last month. — GOING TO (pre-arranged plan)"
📊 You're presenting to the board. The sales data for the last 6 months is all up. Make your forecast.
Model: "Based on these numbers, Q3 is going to be exceptional. — GOING TO (evidence-based prediction)"
💬 Your boss asks: "Do you think the new process will save time?" No data — just your opinion.
Model: "I think it'll probably save around 20% — the manual steps are really slow right now. — WILL (opinion prediction, no hard evidence)"
✉️ The client just asked if someone can handle their account while their manager is on leave.
Model: "I'll cover the account while she's away. — WILL (spontaneous offer)"
📅 In last Monday's meeting, the team decided to move the deadline to the 30th.
Model: "We're going to deliver by the 30th — we agreed that in Monday's meeting. — GOING TO (decision already made)"
😠 A client says: "You've missed three deadlines. Will it happen again?" Make a negative promise.
Model: "It won't happen again. I'll set up a tracking system starting today. — WON'T + WILL (promise)"
☁️ You look at the project timeline and see two tasks are already overdue. Warn your team.
Model: "We're going to miss the deadline if we don't fix this today — look at the board. — GOING TO (evidence visible right now)"
🎭 Layer 2 — Speed pressure: The 3-second rule forces automatic form selection. Students should not be analyzing the rule — they should be feeling it. After all 8 cards: debrief any that caused hesitation. Hesitation = confusion zone = reteach opportunity.
💡 Layer 1 — Common errors: The two trickiest cards are 3 and 4 — both are predictions but one has evidence (going to) and one has opinion only (will). If a student uses the wrong form here, ask: "Do you have data for this? Or is it your feeling?" The question itself teaches the rule.
§5
Solo Challenge — "Business Briefing: Both Forms"
90-sec timed production · ≥3 will sentences · ≥3 going to sentences · ≥1 adverb · b3 badge
⏱ 12 MIN
Brief: You are opening the weekly team meeting. Brief your team on what's planned, what you predict, and what you personally commit to. You have 90 seconds. Use both will and going to — and use them correctly.
≥ 3 WILL sentences
"I'll send... / It'll probably..."
≥ 3 GOING TO sentences
"We're going to launch... / It's going to be..."
≥ 1 adverb
probably / definitely / certainly / likely / possibly
0 Spanish fillers
No "este...", "pues...", "o sea..."
90
Model Briefing (teacher reference only)
"Good morning, team. Quick update on the week. We're going to present the Q1 results to the board on Thursday — the deck is ready. We're also going to meet the Reyes account on Friday to discuss the renewal. On the numbers side: I think revenue will probably close above target this month — we're only three contracts short. The new product launch will definitely attract attention at the conference. On my side: I'll personally follow up with the two at-risk accounts today, and I'll send everyone a summary after this meeting. Any questions? It's going to be a good week."
Tally: will ×4, going to ×4, adverbs: probably ×1, definitely ×1. Above rubric on all counts.
🔥 Layer 3 — Real stakes: Ask the student to use their real company context — what are this week's actual plans? Using real data makes both forms emotionally anchored. A student who produces "I'm going to present to the CFO on Friday" and "I'll handle the client escalation if it comes up" has internalized the contrast, not just drilled it.
💡 Rubric: ≥3 will + ≥3 going to + ≥1 adverb = b3 badge. If a student misses the going to target (most common gap — defaulting to will for everything), isolate one planned activity from their calendar: "That meeting on Thursday — you already know about it, right? So which form is correct?" Self-correction via context is more durable than correction via rule.

Source Analysis — "Team Update Email in the Wild"
Real business email mixing will and going to · Identify form + function · Spot the error
⏱ 8 MIN
📧 Internal Email — Weekly Team Update
Subject: Week 18 Update — Action Items & Forecast

Hi everyone,

Quick recap of this week's priorities and where we stand.

On the planned side: we are going to submit the client proposal on Wednesday — Ana has been finalizing it since Monday. The marketing team is going to launch the new social campaign on Friday, as scheduled.

On the live side: I will call the Mendoza account this afternoon — they just emailed about a delay, so I need to handle it now. I'll send a follow-up summary to the whole team after the call.

Forecast: based on current numbers, Q2 is going to be strong — three deals are in final approval. I think revenue will probably exceed the target by around 8% if both pending contracts close this week.

One more thing: I'll definitely be at the Thursday review. See you all then.

Best,
Carlos Vega · Sales Lead
going to (planned) going to (evidence) will (spontaneous) promise will prediction will
💡 For each highlighted form: (1) Is it planned, spontaneous, a promise, or a prediction? (2) Which form uses evidence from the email? (3) Find the sentence where Carlos used "will" for a planned action — is it an error?
Analysis Key
"are going to submit" — planned (decided days ago, Ana already working on it)
"is going to launch" — planned (scheduled for Friday)
"will call" — spontaneous (triggered by the email arriving just now)
"I'll send a follow-up" — spontaneous offer/promise (decided at moment of writing)
"is going to be strong" — evidence prediction (three deals in final approval = visible data)
"will probably exceed" — opinion prediction (conditional on pending contracts = uncertainty)
"I'll definitely be at the Thursday review" — NOTE: This is technically a pre-planned event (it was already in the diary). A purist would say "I'm going to be there" is more precise. But in native English, "I'll be there" for a confirmed plan is extremely common and acceptable, especially in informal emails. Both forms work here. Discuss with student: "Which sounds more natural to you? Which is more 'correct'?"
Discussion Questions
1. Why does Carlos use going to for the campaign launch but will for calling the Mendoza account? (Campaign was pre-planned; call was triggered by new information — spontaneous)
2. What does "I think... will probably" tell you about Carlos's confidence level? ("I think" = opinion marker; "probably" = moderate confidence adverb — he's NOT certain)
3. Could Carlos have written "I'm going to call the Mendoza account"? What would change? (Yes, but it would imply the call was already planned — which contradicts "they just emailed." Will is more honest here.)
💡 Layer 1 — Key insight: The "I'll definitely be at Thursday's review" is the richest example. It shows why native English doesn't always follow the textbook rule — going to and will overlap for confirmed plans in informal written English. This is A2.1 → B1 awareness: "The rule is real. Native speakers sometimes break it. Knowing when native speakers break it is a B1 skill."
🔥 Layer 3 — Output extension: Challenge: "Write your own weekly update email using at least 2 going to (planned) and 2 will (spontaneous/promise) correctly placed. Use a real situation from your job."

🎯 This week at work
3 Will / Going To Phrases You Can Use Starting Tomorrow
PHRASE 1
"I'll handle that."
The spontaneous offer. Use when something comes up in real time and you step forward. Never say "I'm going to handle that" for a decision made at that exact moment.
PHRASE 2
"We're going to [action] on [date] — it's already scheduled."
The pre-plan signal. Adding "it's already scheduled" or "we decided last week" makes the going to choice feel natural and justified.
PHRASE 3
"Based on [evidence], it's going to be [outcome]."
The evidence prediction template. Leads with your data source, which is what justifies going to over will.
