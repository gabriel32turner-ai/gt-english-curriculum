💡 This seeds the grammar section and the Flash Cards game. Let students debate freely in English or Spanish. Write the comparison language that emerges: "Option A is better because..." / "You should choose B because..." Both M3 (should) and M5 (comparatives) activated simultaneously.
⚠️ Advice or rule? Which is stronger in English? "You should arrive on time." vs "You must arrive on time." vs "You have to arrive on time." What is the difference? Discuss with your partner.
💡 This is the diagnostic question for S3A. Expected answer: must = internal strong rule, have to = external obligation, should = advice/recommendation (not compulsory). If students say all three are the same, that is the error S3A Grammar directly addresses.

§3
Colloquial — Advice Phrases That Sound Natural
4 expressions for giving advice like a native speaker · 5 min
⏱ 5 MIN
"If I were you, I'd [advice]."
The most natural and empathetic way to give advice in English. Puts you in the other person's shoes. More personal and warmer than "you should." Very common in professional and personal conversations.
"If I were you, I'd negotiate the salary before I accepted. You have more power than you think."
"Have you thought about [option]?"
A softer, non-directive way to suggest an idea. Invites the person to consider something new without imposing. Very useful when you are not sure your advice will be welcome.
"Have you thought about asking for a trial period? Some companies accept that before a full commitment."
"It's worth trying."
Encourages someone to take a small risk or make an attempt. Suggests the potential benefit is greater than the risk. Pairs naturally with could: "You could negotiate — it's worth trying."
"You could ask for a higher salary. Honestly, it's worth trying — the worst they can say is no."
"My advice would be to [verb]."
More formal and structured. Use in presentations, professional emails, or when speaking to a client or senior contact. Sounds authoritative and thoughtful.
"My advice would be to take Option B. The growth potential is more important than the salary at this stage of your career."
⚡ Teacher Note — Colloquial Drill
Say: "Click each phrase to hear it — repeat with me." Then run the scenario drill: Andrea has two job offers. Each student uses ONE of the four phrases to give her advice. You cannot use the same phrase as the previous student. This forces variety and production. Anticipated errors: "If I was you" → "If I WERE you" — the subjunctive form "were" is obligatory for all persons in this fixed expression. Teach this as a FIXED CHUNK: memorize "If I were you, I'd" as one unit — don't try to explain the subjunctive at A2.2. "Have you thought about" is followed by -ing or a noun, not infinitive: "Have you thought about NEGOTIATING?" not "to negotiate." "It's worth trying" always -ing after "worth." Cultural note: Mexican business culture tends toward direct advice. "Have you thought about" and "If I were you" teach a more indirect, face-saving register that is standard in international English workplaces.
§4
Grammar — Should, Could & Comparative Advice
Problem → Tool · M3 integrated with M5 comparatives · 12–15 min
⏱ 12–15 MIN
🚨 Real Work Situation
Andrea shows you both job offers. Option A: same company, +20% salary, 60% travel. Option B: new company, -10% salary, no travel, director track in 2 years. She asks: "What do you think I should do?" You want to give her a structured recommendation — using comparatives to justify it, and should/could to deliver it professionally. But in Spanish you say "Yo que tú, agarraría la B porque es mejor." In English you freeze after "I think you should..." and can't explain why.
The problem: giving advice in English requires more than should. A professional recommendation needs: the advice (should/could) + the comparison (more/better/less) + the reason (because/so). Without all three, your advice sounds incomplete.
🔧 The Tool — The Professional Advice Formula
A complete professional recommendation in English has three parts:

1. The advice: "You should choose Option B." / "You could also negotiate the salary first."
2. The comparison: "…because Option B is more flexible than A" / "…the growth potential is better than any option you have now."
3. The reason: "…so you won't have to travel" / "…because your long-term goals align better with B."

Full formula: should/could + comparative + because/so
Example: "You should choose Option B because it is more aligned with your goals, so you won't have to sacrifice your personal life."
Advice StructureStrengthProfessional useExample (Andrea's decision)
should + verbStrong recommendationDirect professional advice; you are fairly confident this is the right choiceYou should accept Option B. The growth trajectory is much better.
could + verbSofter suggestionOffer an alternative or option without imposing; invite considerationYou could also ask Option A to reduce the travel requirement before deciding.
should + comparativeJustified adviceCombine advice with comparison — the professional standard for recommendationsYou should choose B because it is more flexible and better aligned with your goals.
shouldn't + verbStrong negative adviceWarn against a bad decision; more direct than "I don't think you should"You shouldn't decide based on salary alone. The other factors are more important long-term.
⚠️ Common Errors — advice in professional English:
✗ "I recommend you to accept Option B." → ✓ I recommend accepting / You should accept — "recommend + to + infinitive" is wrong; use should or recommend + -ing
✗ "You should to negotiate." → ✓ You should negotiate — never "to" after should (bare infinitive rule applies to ALL modals)
✗ "If I was you, I'd choose B." → ✓ If I were you — fixed chunk; "were" for all persons in this expression
✗ "Option B is more better than A." → ✓ Option B is better than A — never "more" + irregular comparative
⚡ ACTIVITY A — Complete the professional advice

Choose the correct word or phrase to complete each advice sentence. Think about the strength of the advice and the grammar rule.

1. "You _____ accept the first offer without negotiating. Always ask for more — the worst they say is no." [strong negative advice]
2. "You _____ also ask about the bonus structure. It might be more important than the base salary." [soft alternative suggestion]
3. "I recommend _____ the salary before you sign. You have nothing to lose." [recommend + correct form]
4. "You should choose Option B because it is _____ than A for your long-term goals." [comparative + than]
5. "If I _____ you, I'd take the job with the better growth potential — salary can always be negotiated later." [fixed chunk]

⚡ ACTIVITY B — Reorder the recommendation

Drag the parts into the correct order to form a complete, structured professional recommendation.

Sentence 1: (should + comparative + so)

  • ⋮so you won't have to travel every week.
  • ⋮You should accept Option B
  • ⋮because it is more flexible than Option A,

Sentence 2: (shouldn't + reason + could alternative)

  • ⋮You could ask them to reduce the travel requirement first.
  • ⋮You shouldn't decide based on salary alone
  • ⋮because the other factors are more important long-term.
⚡ Teacher Note — Grammar
The Professional Advice Formula (should/could + comparative + because/so) is the conceptual anchor of S3A. Write it on the board and keep it visible the whole session. Activity A key differentiations: Q1 (shouldn't vs mustn't) — shouldn't = advice not to do something; mustn't = strong rule/prohibition. Context "the worst they say is no" is advice, not a rule. Q2 (could vs must) — "also" is the soft suggestion marker; must would be too strong here. Q3 (recommend + -ing) — this is the most common grammar error at A2.2 in professional writing; drill the pattern as a fixed unit. Activity B: after checking, ask "Can you replace the content with a real situation from your company?" This transfer moment is the highest-value 60 seconds of the session.

A2.2 · M6 · S3A · Voice: Sarah (American English) · Speed: 0.82 · ~58 sec
Hi Andrea. I heard you have two job offers. I'm glad you asked me — I've been in this industry for twenty years, so maybe I can help you think it through. My advice would be to choose Option B. I know the salary is lower, but in my experience, growth potential is more important than money at this stage of your career. Option B is more aligned with your long-term goals, and it gives you a clearer path to a director role. That said, you shouldn't just accept it without negotiating. You could ask for a slightly higher starting salary — it's worth trying. They might say yes, and if they don't, you still have a great option on the table. It might sound exciting now, but after a year, it becomes exhausting. Have you thought about talking to someone who works at Company B? They could give you a better perspective from the inside. That's the most useful thing you could do before you decide.

⚡ Teacher Note — Speaking
Q2 is the diagnostic production task: listen for whether students use the full formula (advice + comparative + reason) or stop after the advice alone. The most common gap is the missing reason ("because/so"). Students who produce "you should choose B because it is more flexible so you won't have to travel" are at the target level. Q3 is the highest-value activity: real context from their own company. This is the moment where A2.2 grammar connects to their actual professional lives. Resist the urge to correct during Q3 — collect errors and review after Q4. Q5/Q6 are performance tasks: the mentor role-play is essentially a mini exit task for M6 S3. Scoring: 1pt per advice structure used, 2pts for comparative + reason combination, 3pts for a colloquial phrase. Opinion Meter: the statement is strategically true — almost all students will agree. Use the 10% who disagree to generate discussion: "Give an example of advice without a reason that is MORE effective. When?" (Answer: emergency situations, very close relationships). This deepens their register awareness.
🎯 This week, use these 3 phrases at work:
Professional advice you can give starting tomorrow — any meeting, any email, any conversation.
"You should [advice] because it is more [adj] than [alternative]."
The complete professional recommendation formula. Use it the next time a colleague asks your opinion on a decision. Combines M3 (should) + M5 (comparative) in one natural sentence. Immediately elevates your English from opinion to professional recommendation.
"If I were you, I'd [advice] — it's worth trying."
Combine two colloquial phrases in one sentence. Use when a colleague is hesitant to take action. "If I were you" shows empathy; "it's worth trying" reduces the perceived risk. Works in emails too: "If I were you, I'd send a follow-up — it's worth trying."
"Have you thought about [option]? It might be more [adj] than you think."
Soft suggestion + comparative + possibility (might). Three A2.2 structures in one natural question. Use when you want to propose an alternative without imposing. The softest, most professional way to give unsolicited advice to a senior contact.

Wrap-Up — Session 3A complete
Key takeaways · Checklist · Next session preview · 5–8 min
⏱ 5–8 MIN

💬 The Advice Formula

A professional recommendation needs three parts: the advice (should/could), the comparison (more/better than), and the reason (because/so). Without all three, advice sounds incomplete in professional English.

✍️ Should vs Could

Should = strong recommendation. Could = softer alternative. Shouldn't = strong negative advice. Never say "to" after any of them. Recommend + -ing (not "to"). These distinctions mark the difference between A2.2 and B1 English.

🧠 4 Advice Phrases

"If I were you, I'd..." / "Have you thought about...?" / "It's worth trying." / "My advice would be to..." These four expressions make your advice sound natural, empathetic, and professional in any context.

✅ I CAN NOW...

  • ☐Give a complete professional recommendation using the formula: should/could + comparative + because/so.
  • ☐Distinguish between should (advice), could (soft suggestion), and shouldn't (negative advice) in professional contexts.
  • ☐Use recommend + -ing correctly and avoid the error "recommend to [verb]."
  • ☐Use 4 natural advice phrases: "If I were you," "Have you thought about," "It's worth trying," "My advice would be."
  • ☐Give a 90-second mentor recommendation combining advice + comparison + reason in one natural conversation.
