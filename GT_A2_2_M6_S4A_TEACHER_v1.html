§3
Colloquial — Presenting Conclusions Like a Pro
4 phrases for closing a professional comparison or recommendation · 5 min
⏱ 5 MIN
"All things considered…"
The classic opener for a professional conclusion. Signals that you have evaluated all factors before making your recommendation. Sounds balanced, thoughtful, and authoritative. Use it before your superlative conclusion.
"All things considered, Option B is the most viable choice for our team. The trade-off is worth it."
"The bottom line is…"
Gets straight to the most important point after a longer comparison. Signals "everything else is detail — this is what matters most." Extremely common in business presentations and meetings.
"The bottom line is: Option A is more expensive and less flexible. We should go with B."
"When you weigh it all up…"
A sophisticated version of "in conclusion." Connects to the comparison process itself — implies you have carefully balanced pros and cons. Pairs naturally with comparatives and superlatives.
"When you weigh it all up, Option B gives us the best outcome at the most competitive cost."
"It's a no-brainer."
Use when the comparison is so clear that the decision seems obvious. Works best after you have already presented the evidence. Casual but very impactful — shows confidence. Already seen in M5 — here it integrates with superlatives.
"Option B is the most efficient AND the most affordable. Honestly, it's a no-brainer."
⚡ Teacher Note — Colloquial
Say: "Click each phrase to hear it — repeat with me." Then run the mini-challenge: each student uses ONE phrase to close their superlative sentence from the warm-up. "All things considered, this is the best decision." Drill the specific word stress: "all THINGS con-SI-dered" / "the BOT-tom line IS" / "when you WEIGH it all UP." The stress pattern is what makes these sound natural vs robotic. Cultural note: "No-brainer" returned from M5 S1B — point this out as deliberate. Students who recognize it from M5 are demonstrating cross-session vocabulary consolidation. "All things considered" is a complete sentence opener — always followed by a comma, then the conclusion. It cannot end a sentence.
§4
Grammar — Superlatives + Full A2.2 Integration
Problem → Tool · Superlatives review + integration with all M6 structures · 12–15 min
⏱ 12–15 MIN
🚨 Real Work Situation
Your manager asks you to present a comparison of three suppliers to the whole team. You can describe each one with adjectives and comparatives. But when you try to name "the best one overall," you freeze. In Spanish you say "es el más eficiente de los tres." In English you try "it is the most efficient of the three" — but then you add "and the most better option" and lose confidence. You know comparatives well. Superlatives feel unpredictable.
The problem: superlatives and comparatives look similar but follow different rules. And when you try to combine them with should, might, used to, or going to in one sentence, the result sounds like a grammar exercise — not a professional presentation.
🔧 The Tool — Superlatives + A2.2 Integration Formula
Superlatives describe the extreme point of a comparison (among 3 or more options):

Short adjectives: the + adj + -est → fast → the fastest / cheap → the cheapest
Long adjectives: the most + adj → reliable → the most reliable / efficient → the most efficient
Irregular: good → the best / bad → the worst / far → the farthest

Now integrate with A2.2 structures:
"This is the most reliable supplier we used to work with — but it's no longer the cheapest." (M1 + M5)
"You should choose the supplier with the best delivery record because it might save you time." (M3 + M4 + M5)
"We are going to work with the most competitive option in the market." (M4 + M5)
Adjective typeComparativeSuperlativeBusiness example
Short (1 syllable)cheaper / faster / biggerthe cheapest / the fastest / the biggestThis is the cheapest option and also the fastest to implement.
Ends in -yheavier / busier / easierthe heaviest / the busiest / the easiestThe busiest quarter is Q4 — we should prepare the most reliable team for it.
Long (2+ syllables)more reliable / more efficientthe most reliable / the most efficientThis is the most efficient system we have ever used. The outcome has been the best.
Irregularbetter / worse / fartherthe best / the worst / the farthestThis is the best decision I have made and the worst I could delay any longer.
⚠️ Common Errors — superlatives in professional English:
✗ "It is the most best option." → ✓ It is the best option — NEVER "most" + irregular superlative
✗ "This is the most fast delivery." → ✓ the fastest delivery — short adjectives use -est, not "most"
✗ "She is the more experienced candidate of the three." → ✓ the most experienced — use superlative when comparing 3+ options
✗ "This has been our greater challenge." → ✓ our greatest challenge — superlative, not comparative
⚡ ACTIVITY A — Comparative or superlative?

Choose the correct form. Read the context clue: are there 2 options (comparative) or 3+ (superlative)?

1. "We evaluated five suppliers. In the end, Lumina Express was _____ for our needs." [5 suppliers = superlative]
2. "Between Option A and Option B, Option B is _____ for long-term growth." [2 options = comparative]
3. "This has been _____ project in my career. I learned more than in any other role." [superlative + irregular]
4. "All things considered, this is _____ trade-off we have faced this year." [superlative + irregular]
5. "You should choose the supplier with _____ response rate — it might make the biggest impact on client satisfaction." [superlative + integrated M3+M4]
🗣️
Micro-Speaking: Say one integrated sentence using a superlative + one other A2.2 structure. Example: "The most important decision I am going to make this year is choosing the right supplier — I should weigh up all the criteria before I decide." Use YOUR real work context.
⚡ ACTIVITY B — Reorder the integrated conclusion

Drag the parts into the correct order to form a complete professional conclusion using superlatives + A2.2 structures.

Sentence 1: (superlative + used to + now + conclusion)

  • ⋮but now it is the most efficient system we have.
  • ⋮All things considered, this is the best decision we could have made.
  • ⋮We used to have the slowest processing time in the region,

Sentence 2: (should + superlative + might + conclusion)

  • ⋮When you weigh it all up, it is the most viable option we have.
  • ⋮because it might also reduce the most significant costs.
  • ⋮You should choose the supplier with the best track record
⚡ Teacher Note — Grammar
S4A grammar is fundamentally a consolidation and elevation session. Students have seen superlatives in M5 — S4A integrates them with the four other A2.2 structures learned in M6. The key pedagogical move is making students FEEL the difference between comparative (2 options: A vs B) and superlative (3+ options: which is the best overall?). Activity A context clues are designed to make this explicit: Q1 says "five suppliers" (superlative); Q2 says "between Option A and B" (comparative). Activity B Sentence 1 establishes the full A2.2 integration pattern: M1 (used to) + M5 (superlative) + conclusion phrase. Sentence 2 adds M3 (should) + M4 (might) + M5 (superlative). After checking, ask: "How many A2.2 structures appeared in Sentence 2?" (3: should, might, superlative). Students who can count them are ready for S5 exit task.

Andrea's 3-Month Review — Company B, León
A2.2 · M6 · S4A · Voice: Sarah (American English) · Speed: 0.82 · ~60 sec
Good morning. My name is Andrea Soto and I am the new Regional Sales Coordinator at Company B in León. I used to work at Vertex Logistics in Celaya. Back in the day, I managed a smaller territory and had the highest travel commitment of anyone on the team — almost sixty percent. Now I am responsible for the largest sales portfolio in the Bajío region, and I work from one office. It is the best trade-off I have ever made. When I compared both offers three months ago, my mentor suggested I should choose the role with the most long-term potential. He was right. Company B has given me the most rewarding professional experience of my career so far. Going forward, we are going to expand to three more cities. I might also take on a director position if the results continue. The bottom line is: I made the most important decision of my career, and all things considered, it was a no-brainer. If you are facing a similar choice right now, my advice would be to look beyond the salary. The most viable option is not always the most obvious one. Thank you.

S-S Question 3 — Integrate
Your partner presents a work problem. You give a structured recommendation using the full A2.2 toolkit: used to + should + superlative + might. Example: "You used to have the best supplier. You should find the most reliable one now because the current option might not be viable."

Class Question 4 — Debate
Which is more important in a professional decision: the best salary, the most growth potential, or the most flexible schedule? Each student makes a case using superlatives + at least one other A2.2 structure. Then class votes.

S4A Speaking is the integration summit of the whole M6 arc. Q3 is the key integration task: 4 different A2.2 structures in 2–3 connected sentences. Scoring rubric for the Comparison Pitch: 1pt per A2.2 structure used, 2pts per superlative with correct form, 3pts for a colloquial phrase used naturally, 5pts for a complete recommendation with all three elements (superlative + because + so). 20+ points = exit project ready. Opinion Meter: the statement is strategically contentious — superlatives can sound overconfident without evidence. Students who disagree are right too — this generates the best discussion. 🎯 This week, use these 3 phrases at work:
Professional English you can use in any meeting, email, or presentation starting tomorrow.
"All things considered, [option] is the most [adj] choice because [reason]."
Your complete professional conclusion in one sentence. Use it to close any comparison or recommendation at work: supplier selection, candidate evaluation, project decision. The "all things considered" opener signals that you have been thorough, not impulsive.
"The bottom line is: [superlative conclusion]."
Use after a longer comparison to land your main point. Very powerful in meetings when you want to cut through details and give a clear recommendation. Example: "The bottom line is: Option B is the most viable long-term and we should move forward."
"When you weigh it all up, [option] has the most [adj] outcome."
Combines the comparison process ("weigh it up" from S3A vocabulary) with a superlative conclusion. Use in written reports, emails, or verbal presentations when you want to sound structured and analytical.
