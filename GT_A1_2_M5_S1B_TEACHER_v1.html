2
Speaking Topic — Email Production Rounds
Write → Read → Evaluate · Peer feedback format
10–12 min
T-S · Guided Write
Situation: You are an operations coordinator at Solaris Manufacturing, Celaya. Write a 4-sentence email to your supplier to confirm a delivery next Tuesday at 7 AM. Include all 5 email parts. 2 minutes.
Subject + "Dear…" + "I'm writing to confirm…" + 1 detail sentence + "Best regards"
"Subject: Delivery Confirmation — Tuesday, June 3rd. Dear Mr. Vega, I'm writing to confirm the delivery scheduled for Tuesday, June 3rd at 7 AM. Please arrive at Gate 3 with your delivery documents. Thank you for your service. Best regards, [Your name] · Operations Coordinator · Solaris Manufacturing."
S-S · Peer Review
Exchange your email with your partner. Check: (1) Is the subject line present? (2) Does the opening say "I'm writing to…"? (3) Is there a sign-off with name and title? Give one piece of written feedback.
Say: "Your email is good because… One suggestion: …"
"Your email is good because the subject line is clear and the opening is professional. One suggestion: add your title after your name in the sign-off — 'Best regards, Ana García · Operations Coordinator.'"
Class · Read & Evaluate
3 volunteers read their email aloud. Class evaluates together: Did it have all 5 parts? Was the opening professional? Would you send this to a real client?
Evaluation format: Thumbs up for each of the 5 parts as you hear them
5-part checklist: ✅ Subject line — clear and professional? ✅ Greeting — "Dear" for formal, "Hi" for semi-formal? ✅ Opening — "I'm writing to…"? ✅ Body — one clear idea per sentence? ✅ Sign-off — "Best/Kind regards" + name + title?
TEACHER NOTE — S1B Speaking is Writing + Speaking

In S1B, speaking practice IS writing practice — students write, then read aloud. This forces them to own their production. The peer review (Round 2) is the most valuable moment: students must articulate what makes an email professional. Walk the room during Round 1 — correct sign-offs in real time. Common omission: students write name but forget their job title. Remind: "In international business, your title tells the reader your authority level. Always include it."

4
Colloquial — Apply in Real Situations
From S1A: Got it · On it · Sounds good · Thanks a lot
5 min
Remember from S1A → Now Apply in Context
Situation 1 — Your manager sends you a task list by email
What do you reply? Use 2 of the 4 colloquial phrases.
Model: "Got it. On it — I'll have everything ready by end of day. Thanks a lot for the clear instructions."
Situation 2 — A colleague proposes Friday at 3 PM for a meeting
You agree. Reply in 1 sentence using a colloquial phrase.
Model: "Sounds good — Friday at 3 PM works perfectly. See you then."
Situation 3 — A supplier sends you the invoice you requested
Acknowledge receipt in 1 sentence. Use a colloquial phrase.
Model: "Got it — thanks a lot for sending it so quickly. I'll process it today."
TEACHER NOTE — Colloquial Application

S1B colloquial is application, not introduction. Run Situation 1 as a class (all students write simultaneously), then Situations 2-3 as rapid-fire with individual students. Time box: 60 seconds per situation. The key insight: these 4 phrases work together — a typical email reply chain might use all 4 in one message. Demonstrate: "Got it. On it — I'll confirm by Friday. Sounds good for the timeline. Thanks a lot for the update." Ask: "Does this feel too informal for your company?" Generate discussion about register. Most Bajío companies accept this in internal emails.

TEACHER NOTE — Grammar Activities

Activity A (unjumble) is active grammar manipulation — it forces students to think about word order without a model sentence to copy. Run both unjumbles simultaneously in pairs: first pair to check both correctly earns the badge. Key error in Activity B: students write "What is the agenda for Friday meeting?" — missing the article: "Friday's meeting" or "the Friday meeting." Quick board correction. Activity B peer email review is the S1B payoff — students simultaneously produce AND evaluate, which is the highest level of language awareness at A1.2.

Email Chain — Client Meeting Request · ConnectPro International, Guadalajara
A1.2 · M5 · S1B · Voice: Sarah (American English) · Speed: 0.80
ElevenLabs Script — ~158 words · ~59 seconds at 0.80

Hello. My name is Mónica Herrera, Sales Manager at ConnectPro International. Today I want to read you a two-email exchange. Listen for the structure and the Wh-questions.

Email 1 — from Mónica to a client:
Subject: Meeting Request — Q3 Partnership. Dear Mr. Nakamura, I'm writing to request a meeting to discuss our Q3 partnership proposal. Are you available next week? What day works best for you? The meeting will be approximately one hour. Please find attached our presentation for your review. I look forward to hearing from you. Best regards, Mónica Herrera · Sales Manager · ConnectPro International.

Email 2 — reply from Mr. Nakamura:
Dear Mónica, Thank you for your email. Sounds good — I'm available on Wednesday at 10 AM or Thursday at 2 PM. What is the agenda for the meeting? Also, where is your office — do you have parking? I look forward to our meeting. Best regards, Kenji Nakamura · Procurement Director · Nakamura Industries.

TEACHER NOTE — Listening Strategy S1B

S1B listening is ANALYSIS, not just comprehension. The peer review checklist is the key tool — students verify their own understanding by checking off the elements. After the checklist: ask "Mr. Nakamura used a colloquial phrase at the start of his reply — which one?" (Answer: "Sounds good") Then: "Was his email professional? He used 'Sounds good' with a business contact he doesn't know well — is that appropriate?" Expect debate. Answer: yes — it softens the reply and acknowledges the meeting positively. Register discussion at A1.2 = high-value output. Badge b4 awarded when all 10 checklist items are confirmed correct.

TEACHER NOTE — Speaking Practice S1B

Q2 + Q3 (write → peer review) is the S1B core — 8 minutes of the 12. Walk the room during Q2. Collect 3 different email types (different subjects/purposes) for Q4 class evaluation. The class annotation of a projected email is the most visible grammar lesson of the module — students see a real peer production corrected in real time. Q5 (reply) is the chain-completion activity — by writing both the request AND the reply, students experience the full professional communication cycle. Q6 (60-second recall) reveals who has truly internalized S1A. No partial credit — all 5 parts + one phrase each = full marks.
