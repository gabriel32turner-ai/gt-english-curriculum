⚡ Hot Take  ·  5 min

Celebrity Culture — Final Position

“Celebrities who use their platform for social causes must be applauded — unless the cause conveniently improves their own image.”
Voted. Apply Carlos’s 3 questions to justify your position: whose interest? what are we being sold? what’s the comparison?
This Hot Take integrates Carlos’s 3 questions from S3A directly into S4B — cross-session conceptual transfer. The “impact vs intent” distinction is a genuinely contested philosophical position. Learners who argue “impact must matter more” are usually making the stronger B1.2 argument because it forces them to produce evidence-based reasoning, not just moral assertions. Enforce: every claim must include a modal deduction or Carlos’s framework.
🗣 Pitch Round  ·  10 min

The Endorsement Contract Negotiation

A celebrity has been offered 4 different endorsement contracts. Each pair argues why their contract must be accepted — or why the celebrity can’t sign it ethically. 2 min prep — 2 min pitch.

CONTRACT A — FAST FOOD CHAIN
A global fast food brand. $8 million. The celebrity has been public about healthy eating and fitness. The deal requires 12 social media posts per year. Their fans are primarily teenagers. No disclosure of the fee is required by contract.
Argue for OR against. Must use: persona, image, endorse, audience, modal deduction
CONTRACT B — LUXURY CAR BRAND
A premium electric vehicle manufacturer. $4 million. The celebrity is known for environmental activism. The brand has strong sustainability credentials. However, their cars are unaffordable for 99% of the celebrity’s followers.
Argue for OR against. Must use: brand, image, credibility, obsession, modal deduction
CONTRACT C — A POLITICAL PARTY
A centrist political party ahead of national elections. No fee — the celebrity genuinely believes in the platform. But they have followers across all political spectrums. A political endorsement might alienate half their audience permanently.
Argue for OR against. Must use: fame, persona, fan, brand, modal deduction
CONTRACT D — A CHARITY
A major mental health charity. No payment. The celebrity has spoken openly about their own struggles with mental health. But their publicist says the association might “damage the aspirational image.” The charity is desperate for the celebrity’s reach.
Argue for OR against. Must use: image, obsession, privacy, inspire, modal deduction
PREP TIME
PITCH TIME
Contract D (charity / mental health) is the most emotionally complex and usually generates the strongest language. The publicist’s phrase “damage the aspirational image” is worth unpacking with the group: what must “aspirational image” mean, and who does it serve? Contract C (political endorsement) is the most contentious — in Mexico, celebrity political endorsements carry real cultural weight. Handle with sensitivity but don’t avoid it: the language it generates is authentically B1.2.
🏃 Relay  ·  8 min

Exclusive Access Pass Relay

THE SITUATION: A major celebrity has just announced a surprise retirement from the spotlight. No explanation. Their team has offered 4 types of “exclusive access” to understand what’s happening. Each learner holds one access pass — and must argue from that perspective using modal deduction.
PASS TYPE A
👥 The Fan
You’ve followed them for 10 years. You must be devastated. The retirement can’t be voluntary — something must have happened. You have access to every public statement they’ve ever made.
PASS TYPE B
📰 The Journalist
You have a 10-minute interview. You can ask 3 questions. What must you ask? The public deserve the truth — but the celebrity’s privacy can’t be violated either.
PASS TYPE C
🏭 The Brand
You have a $12 million endorsement contract that runs another 2 years. The retirement must affect your campaign. Can’t cancel without legal consequences. What must you do?
PASS TYPE D
🎤 The Publicist
You know the full story. You can’t share it. The retirement might be temporary — or permanent. Your job is to manage public narrative without lying. The hardest role in celebrity culture.
Relay rule: Each learner speaks for 45 seconds from their access level. The next learner must begin: “From [my perspective], what [previous speaker] said must be true — but what they can’t know from their position is…” This forces perspective-aware modal deduction across all four passes.
45s RELAY TIMER
The Exclusive Access Pass is S4B’s unique format — distinct from S1B (Platform cards), S2B (Editing Room), and S3B (Source Chain). Pass D (Publicist) is the most demanding because it requires speaking authoritatively while deliberately withholding information — this is advanced English for professional communication under constraint. The relay rule (“what they can’t know from their position”) forces epistemic awareness: not just what we think, but what our position allows us to know. This is rare at B1.2 and worth celebrating when it appears.
⚡ Speed Round  ·  6 min

30 Seconds — Celebrity Culture Edition

Apply Carlos’s 3 questions to a celebrity you admire. Whose interest does following them serve? What are you being sold? What are you comparing yourself to?
Honest self-reflection + modal deduction = the highest B1.2 production
A celebrity you admire just made a public statement you strongly disagree with. What must you do as a fan? What can’t you ignore? What might change your mind?
All 3 modals in one argument. Personal + analytical.
Is there a difference between “idolizing” a celebrity and “admiring” them? Use modal deduction to explain where the line must be.
idolize = must place on unrealistic level; admire = must remain grounded
Describe a celebrity who must have been better WITHOUT fame — and one who must have needed it. What does each case reveal about the relationship between talent and public attention?
Comparative must have — past deduction about different trajectories
If your company suddenly became famous because of a viral social media moment, what must your communications team do in the first 24 hours?
Professional + must / can’t + brand, image, persona vocabulary
In 30 seconds, make the strongest possible argument that celebrity culture is net positive for society. Use evidence and modal deduction.
Steelman: find the best version of the argument you might not believe
30s SPEED TIMER
Card 6 (steelman) is the most intellectually demanding card in any M5 B-session Speed Round — it requires learners to argue sincerely for a position they may not hold. This is authentic critical thinking and advanced English production. Card 5 (viral company moment) is the most professionally transferable. Card 1 (Carlos’s 3 questions, personal) closes the module’s reflective arc: learners apply the framework to themselves, not just to media.
🌟 Solo Challenge  ·  8 min

The Celebrity PR Crisis Statement

Your Scenario
Your celebrity client has just been photographed at a private event that contradicts their public persona entirely. The photos are trending. In 90 seconds, you must give a public statement that acknowledges the situation, uses modal deduction to frame what must and might have happened, and protects the long-term brand without lying.

This is the hardest job in communications — and you must do it in English, live, under pressure.
✅ Use must have / might have for what happened
✅ Use can’t be for what the situation is NOT
✅ Use at least 4 S4A vocabulary words
✅ End with a forward-looking statement that rebuilds image
❌ No notes. No reading. No apology that admits guilt without strategy.
90s SOLO TIMER
Crisis PR language uses modal deduction as its primary tool: framing what happened, what didn’t happen, and what’s uncertain. This is the most professionally transferable solo challenge of M5.

🎯 3 Phrases From This Session — Use Them This Week:
“Their persona can’t survive this if it goes public.”
Use it when assessing reputational risk for a person, brand, or company. Professional and analytical. Works in risk management, HR, and communications contexts.
“Whose interest does this endorsement serve?”
The final Carlos question, applied commercially. Use in any meeting where a partnership, sponsorship, or public commitment is being proposed. Forces a clarity that most meetings avoid.
“That’s so extra — and it must be intentional.”
Combines M5 colloquial with modal deduction. Use when analysing an over-the-top public moment — in meetings, in media analysis, or in casual conversation. Shows cultural awareness + analytical thinking.
