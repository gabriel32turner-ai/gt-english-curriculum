§1
Module Quick-Fire — One question per session
Activate memory of S1–S4 content · No notes · 8 min
⏱ 8 MIN
S1 Recall — Networking
Say a complete professional introduction using: used to + responsible for + going to + one networking closer. 30 seconds. No notes.
S2 Recall — Career Arc
In one sentence: past (used to) + present (responsible for) + future (going to) + one S2A colloquial phrase. 15 seconds. Go.
S3 Recall — Advice Formula
Give a piece of advice using the full S3A formula: should/could + comparative + because/so. Use a real situation from your work. Include one S3A colloquial phrase.
S4 Recall — Superlatives
Make one sentence integrating: a superlative + used to + going to + one S4A conclusion phrase. This is the full M6 integration in one sentence.
The Quick-Fire is diagnostic, not evaluative. It shows you which session content is most and least retained before the exit task. QF1 (networking intro) is the most practiced and usually the strongest. QF4 (full integration sentence) is the hardest and reveals who has truly internalized M6. Students who complete QF4 correctly in 15 seconds without notes are performing at the S5 ceiling — note them. Students who blank on QF2 or QF3 have a session-specific gap: target that session in the grammar review (§5). Do NOT give the answers during the quick-fire — let gaps remain open. The S5 exit task is where they close them.

Speaking Integrated — 3 Rounds, Full A2.2
Combines all M6 grammar + vocabulary + colloquials · 10 min
⏱ 10 MIN
T-SRound 1 — The Complete Career Pitch (2 min)
Deliver your complete A2.2 career pitch in 2 minutes using all five M6 structures: used to (S1) + responsible for (S1) + going to / might (S2) + should + comparative (S3) + a superlative conclusion (S4). Use at least 2 colloquial phrases from any session. This is the S5 warm-up.
S-SRound 2 — The Advice & Comparison Exchange (2 min)
Student A presents a real work comparison (two options). Student B gives structured advice using: the most [adj] option + should + comparative + because/so + one S4A conclusion phrase. Then switch. This combines S3 (advice) + S4 (superlatives + conclusions) in one dialogue.
ClassRound 3 — The M6 Final Debate (3 min)
Class debate: "The most important skill A2.2 gave you for your professional life in the Bajío region is [structure]." Each student defends a different M6 structure. Must use the structure they defend + at least one superlative + one conclusion phrase. Class votes: most integrated argument wins 10 points.
2-MINUTE SPEAKING TIMER
Round 1 is the S5 exit task in miniature: 2-minute career pitch with 5 structures. Use it to calibrate who is ready. Round 2 generates the richest pre-exit-task language: real comparisons + real advice = authentic production. Track who produces the superlative + advice formula combination spontaneously (no prompting) — those students are at or above A2.2 ceiling and have B1 readiness. Round 3 generates conviction-driven language, the most fluent kind. The class vote for "most integrated argument" is the self-assessment moment of S5: students evaluate each other against the full M6 grammar set.

Colloquial Review — All M6 Phrases
16 phrases from S1–S4 · Poll: which was most useful? · 6 min
⏱ 6 MIN
S1A
"That’s a great opportunity."
Use to respond positively to new professional contact or information. Closes a conversation with energy.
S1A
"Let’s stay in touch."
Standard professional closer. Always follow with “I’ll reach out next week” to make it concrete.
S1A
"It was great meeting you."
The warmest of the four networking closers. Use in face-to-face events.
S1A
"I’ll reach out next week."
The only networking closer with a time commitment. The most professional of the four.
S2A
"Back in the day…"
Opens a career comparison between past and present. More natural than “In the past.”
S2A
"I’m moving up."
Confident career update. Use when you want to signal growth without sounding arrogant.
S2A
"It’s a stepping stone."
Frames a less-than-perfect role positively. Signals strategic thinking about career trajectory.
S2A
"I’m looking forward to it."
Signals enthusiasm about a future plan. Always sounds positive, professional, and warm.
S3A
"If I were you, I’d…"
The most empathetic advice opener. Always uses “were” — never “was.”
S3A
"Have you thought about…?"
Softest advice form. Use with seniors or sensitive situations. Followed by -ing or a noun.
S3A
"It’s worth trying."
Encourages action when someone hesitates. Pairs naturally with could: “You could try — it’s worth it.”
S3A
"My advice would be to…"
Formal recommendation opener. Use in presentations, reports, or with clients.
S4A
"All things considered…"
Opens a professional conclusion. Signals you have evaluated all factors before recommending.
S4A
"The bottom line is…"
Cuts through detail to land the key point. Very effective in meetings or after a long comparison.
S4A
"When you weigh it all up…"
Connects the conclusion to the comparison process itself. Sounds analytical and structured.
S4A
"It’s a no-brainer."
Use when evidence is overwhelming. Signals confident, decided thinking. Casual but impactful.
👥 Class Poll — Which phrase will YOU use most at work?
The poll reveals which colloquial set resonated most with this particular group. This is valuable marketing intelligence: a class that votes predominantly for S3A advice phrases may have professional contexts where giving recommendations in English is a pain point. Capture this. The click-to-hear feature activates auditory memory — students who hear the phrase pronounced correctly immediately after reviewing it retain it longer. Run the drill: one student says a phrase, the next student has to use it in a sentence about their work within 5 seconds. Chain the whole class. Any hesitation beyond 5 seconds = the phrase is still passive vocabulary for that student.

Grammar Review — All M6 Structures + Error Correction
Summary table · Error correction · Integration formula · 12 min
⏱ 12 MIN
ModuleStructureKey ruleIntegration example (S5 level)
M1 (S1A)used to + verbPast habit/state. No present form. No "use to" (bare). Use but now to contrast."I used to manage a small territory, but now I am responsible for the whole region."
M3 (S3A)should/could + verbNo "to" after modals. Recommend + -ing (NOT "to"). shouldn't = advice not to; mustn't = strong rule."You should choose Option B because it is more aligned with your goals, so you won't have to travel."
M4 (S2A)going to / might / willGoing to = confirmed plan. Might = 30–60% possible. Will = spontaneous decision NOW."We are going to expand to Leon, and we might also open in Queretaro if the results support it."
M5 (S4A)Comparatives + SuperlativesShort adj: -er/-est. Long adj: more/the most. Never "the most best." Compare 2 = comparative; 3+ = superlative."Option B is the most reliable choice we have — it is faster than A and better than any option we have tried."
M6 IntegrationAll five structures combinedUse connectors: but, because, so, and. One sentence can carry 3+ structures naturally."We used to have the worst delivery time, but now we are going to become the most efficient supplier, which should make us more competitive than before."
❌ Error Correction — Click to reveal the fix
✗ "I use to work in Irapuato." [S1A error]
✓ "I used to work in Irapuato." — The past tense is always used to, never use to. There is no present form of this expression.
✗ "You shouldn't to send that email without checking first." [S3A error]
✓ "You shouldn't send that email without checking." — Never "to" after shouldn't (or any modal). Bare infinitive only.
✗ "I recommend to negotiate before you sign." [S3A error]
✓ "I recommend negotiating before you sign." — Recommend is always followed by -ing, never "to + verb."
✗ "This is the most best supplier we have ever worked with." [S4A error]
✓ "This is the best supplier we have ever worked with." — Never "most" with an irregular superlative. Best, worst, farthest are already superlatives.
✗ "If I was you, I'd choose Option B." [S3A error]
✓ "If I were you, I'd choose Option B." — "If I were you" is a fixed chunk — always "were" for all persons.
✗ "I might probably change roles next year." [S2A error]
✓ "I might change roles next year." — Never "might probably" — double uncertainty markers cancel each other. Might already signals possibility.
The integration table is the visual memory anchor for S5. Point to each row and ask: "Which module? Which session?" Students who can identify the module correctly have consolidated the learning architecture. Error correction is a performance activity: read each wrong sentence aloud with a confident voice (as if you believe it is correct), then pause and look around. Students who catch it immediately are at the A2.2 ceiling. Those who need the reveal have passive recognition. The integration example at the bottom of the table is the S5 exit task target sentence — point to it explicitly: "This is what your exit task will look like. 5 structures, one sentence, real professional content."

Andrea Soto — A2.2 Complete Integration
A2.2 · M6 · S5 Exit Listening · Voice: Sarah · Speed: 0.82 · ~75 sec
Good morning, everyone. My name is Andrea Soto, and I would like to take a few minutes to share my professional journey with you. Back in the day, I used to work in customer service in Irapuato. It was a good starting point, but I knew it was just a stepping stone. Now I am responsible for regional sales at Company B in León — the largest territory I have ever managed. When I was choosing between two job offers last year, my mentor advised me well. He said I should focus on long-term growth rather than immediate salary, because a more aligned role is more valuable than a higher paycheck. If I were him, I would have said the same thing. And all things considered, he was right. Now, we are going to expand to three new cities in the Bajío region. I might also take on a director role if the results in León continue. The bottom line is: I made the best decision of my career, and it has opened the most exciting professional chapter I have ever had. To anyone facing a similar choice today: when you weigh it all up, the most important thing is not the salary. It is the opportunity to grow. My advice would be to choose the option that aligns best with who you want to become. It is worth trying. And honestly? It is a no-brainer. Thank you.

Colloquial phrases: back in the day, stepping stone, all things considered, the bottom line is, when you weigh it all up, if I were you, my advice would be to, worth trying, no-brainer. 9 phrases across all 4 sessions. Point out: Andrea uses S3A, S2A, S4A, S1A phrases in ONE monologue — this is the M6 integration target the students are about to demonstrate in their own exit task.

🏆 A2.2 Module 6 · Exit Task
The Professional Integration Pitch
You have 4 minutes. Deliver a complete professional presentation in English using real content from your career and work. Cover all five integration elements:

1. Career arc (S1–S2): Past (used to + back in the day) → Present (responsible for / currently) → Future (going to + might)
2. Professional advice (S3): should/could + comparative + because/so — give advice to your audience about a real work challenge
3. Superlative conclusion (S4): Use at least 3 superlatives to identify your best decision, your most important challenge, or the most valuable thing you learned
4. Colloquial integration (S1–S4): At least 4 colloquial phrases from different sessions — they must appear naturally, not listed
5. Integration sentence: One sentence combining 3+ A2.2 structures with connectors

Use real content. Your real career story. Your real company. Your real decisions. The class listens and scores using the rubric below. The highest scorer is announced as the M6 A2.2 champion and receives the module certificate first.

4-MINUTE EXIT TASK TIMER
The exit task is the culmination of the entire M6 arc. Protocol: (1) Give students 2 minutes to silently organize their thoughts — no writing notes, just thinking. (2) Deal order randomly using the Speaking Cards deck from S4B. (3) Class scores with rubric open on their screens during the pitch. (4) After each pitch, teacher reveals the class average per rubric item in 30 seconds. (5) Announce the score and connect it to the certificate: "12+ out of 15 = A2.2 certified at Module 6 level." Students who score below 9: identify the specific rubric gap and tell them which session to revisit before S5 of the next level. ▶ Coming Next — B1.1
Your A2.2 structures are the foundation. B1.1 adds three new layers.
Everything you learned in A2.2 — your career arc, your advice formula, your comparisons — stays. B1.1 doesn’t replace it. It upgrades it. Three new structures make your English go from professional to internationally fluent:
Present Perfect
"I have worked here for 5 years." Connects your past to now. More precise than used to for ongoing experience. The most important B1 upgrade to your career arc.
First Conditional
"If we choose the best supplier, we will reduce costs." Turns your advice into a consequence analysis. Transforms should into a logical outcome framework.
Passive Voice
"The proposal was approved last week." Essential for professional reporting. Takes your career narrative from personal storytelling to organizational communication.
