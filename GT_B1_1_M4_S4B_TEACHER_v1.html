§1

🔥 Hot Take — Emojis in Professional English

5 min
One opinion. 60 seconds. Use passive voice at least 3 times. No notes.
🔥 Hot Take
"Emojis should be used in professional English messages — they help clarify tone and prevent misunderstandings. A message that might be misread as aggressive is immediately softened by a 🙂. Any professional who refuses to use them is being left behind."
Rule 1: Agree OR disagree — commit fully Rule 2: Passive voice at least 3 times Rule 3: 60 seconds, no notes Rule 4: Use 1 word from S4A vocabulary
Model Answer — Disagree, with nuance

"I disagree — but not completely. Emojis can be used to clarify tone, yes. But I think the risk is the opposite: once emojis are expected, a message sent without one is misread as cold or aggressive. We've created a new problem. Professional communication should be judged by the words, not the symbols. And honestly? Sometimes it's better just to call. No emoji can replace a voice."
■ Vocabulary■ Passive■ Colloquial
Count passives: 4 in the model answer. Ask students how many they used. Award 3 pts for 3+. Bridge: "¿En tu empresa se usan emojis en emails formales?" — passive opportunity: "Are emojis used in your company's formal communications?"
§2

⚡ Pitch / Debate — Should Emojis Be Used Professionally?

10 min
Two sides. Five arguments each. Every argument uses passive voice.
🟣 Side A — Emojis Should Be Allowed
"Tone cannot always be expressed in words alone — emojis should be used to prevent messages from being misread."
  • Tone is often lost in written messages — emojis help fill the gap that would otherwise be created.
  • Conflicts are regularly caused by messages that were misread as aggressive — a single emoji can prevent this.
  • Professional norms are being updated — emojis are now used in major corporations worldwide, including LinkedIn and Slack.
  • The nuance of voice is replaced by emojis in text — without them, important emotional context is lost.
  • If a message might be misinterpreted, a 🙂 signals that no offense was intended.
🟢 Side B — Emojis Should Be Kept Out
"Professional communication should be judged by its words — if context needs to be added, a call should be made."
  • Once emojis are expected, a message sent without one is read as cold — a new problem has been created.
  • Formal documents, HR emails, and client proposals should not be decorated — they are judged by different standards.
  • The meaning of emojis is interpreted differently across cultures — conflict can be caused by a single misread symbol.
  • If clarity is needed, a call should be made — emojis are a workaround, not a solution.
  • Professional credibility is damaged when emojis are overused in contexts where precision is required.
"Emojis should be regulated in professional settings…" "Tone is often misread without context…" "Messages are sent without enough nuance…" "No offense was intended, but it was misread as…"
How to run: 90 sec per side, 30 sec rebuttal each. Award 1 pt per passive used naturally. Point out the present perfect passives ("has been created," "have been updated") — these are B1.1+ structures.

🎤 Relay — Transform to Passive (Communication Context)

10 min
Active to Passive. Digital communication context. Say your answer before revealing.
Revealed: 0 / 5
Card 1 — Past Passive
Active: "Sandra misunderstood Rodrigo's message completely."
→ Transform. Start with "Rodrigo's message…"
✅ "Rodrigo's message was misunderstood by Sandra." — Past passive. Note: keep "by Sandra" here because it identifies who misunderstood — important in a conflict situation. Dropping "by" would lose accountability.
Card 2 — Present Passive Negative
Active: "People don't always consider tone when they send messages."
→ Transform to present passive negative. Start with "Tone…"
✅ "Tone is not always considered when messages are sent." — Two present passives in one sentence. This is a very natural professional statement — common in HR policies and company guidelines.
Card 3 — Modal Passive
Active: "Companies should re-read all sensitive messages before sending them."
→ Transform with modal passive. "should be…"
✅ "All sensitive messages should be re-read before they are sent." — Modal passive: should/must/can + be + past participle. This is the most common form in company policy documents and HR guidelines.
Card 4 — Future Passive
Active: "The company will introduce new digital communication guidelines next quarter."
→ Transform to future passive. Start with "New digital communication guidelines…"
✅ "New digital communication guidelines will be introduced by the company next quarter." — Future passive. "by the company" can be dropped if context is clear: "Guidelines will be introduced next quarter." — equally correct and more natural.
Card 5 — Challenge: Past Perfect Passive
Active: "Nobody had reviewed the email before Rodrigo sent it."
→ Challenge! Past perfect passive: had been + p.p. Start with "The email…"
✅ "The email had not been reviewed by anyone before it was sent." — Past perfect passive: had + been + past participle. This is B1.2 territory — but strong B1.1 students can attempt it. It describes an action that DIDN'T happen before another past event.
Card 5 note: Past perfect passive is B1.2. Name it, write it on the board: "had been + p.p." Don't require it — but strong students who produce it should get bonus points. Award 3 pts for Card 5 vs 2 for Cards 1–4.

🎤 Solo Challenge — 2 Minutes

8 min
Two minutes. Your company's digital communication culture. No notes. Passive voice required.
🎤 SOLO — Your Company's Digital Communication
Describe how digital communication works at your company — and what problems have been caused. Include:
What tools are used to communicate? What rules are expected to be followed? What conflict has been caused by a message? What could be improved or changed? Passive voice minimum 3 times
Scoring: 3 pts — correct passive structures (min 3) · 2 pts — vocabulary from S4A (tone, conflict, misunderstanding, context, clarify) · 2 pts — specific real-world example · 1 pt — fluency · 1 pt — colloquial phrase used naturally. Max: 9 pts.
Model opener: "In my company, WhatsApp is used for most internal communication. Messages are sent constantly — even after hours. A conflict was caused last year when a message was misread by the operations team. The problem could have been avoided if the person had called instead."

🔍 Source Analysis — Digital Communication Around the World

8 min
Four real-world perspectives on digital communication. Find the passive. Answer the questions.
📋 Corporate Policy — Tech Company (adapted)
"All external communications must be reviewed by a manager before they are sent. Tone must be considered carefully. Messages that are perceived as aggressive or unprofessional will be flagged by the compliance team."
❓ Who reviews external communications before they are sent?
🏭 Industry Report — Bajío Region (adapted)
"In plants across Celaya and Querétaro, WhatsApp is used as the primary internal communication tool. Messages are sent at all hours. Conflicts are regularly reported when tone is misread — particularly between engineers and managers from different companies."
❓ When are conflicts most commonly reported?
🎓 Communication Research (adapted)
"Studies show that 55% of communication is expressed through body language — which is completely lost in text. The remaining 38% is expressed through tone of voice — which is also lost. What remains in a text message is approximately 7% of the original communication."
❓ What percentage of communication is preserved in a text message?
💬 LinkedIn Post — Mexican Engineer (adapted)
"A colleague was nearly fired because of a WhatsApp message. The message was misread as insubordination. It was reviewed by three managers. It was eventually explained in a face-to-face meeting. The problem was caused by three words and a missing emoji. Was it worth it?"
❓ How was the conflict eventually resolved?
Passive count challenge: Ask students to count all passive structures across the 4 sources. There are at least 18. Then ask: which ones could drop "by" without losing meaning? This links source analysis to the grammar lesson from S4A.
Discussion hook: Source 4 — "Was it worth it?" Ask students: ¿Han vivido una situación similar en su empresa? — passive opportunity: "Have you ever been misunderstood in a message?"

🎯 3 Phrases for This Week
PHRASE 1
"That message came across wrong."
Use the moment you sense a message has been misread — before conflict escalates. Acknowledges the problem without blaming either person. Works in English and when explained to Spanish-speaking colleagues.
PHRASE 2
"I re-read before I send."
Use this when describing your professional communication habits. Signals emotional intelligence and deliberate communication practice. Contains vocabulary from S4A in natural, real-world context.
PHRASE 3
"Sometimes it's better just to call."
The most practical phrase from S4. Use when messages are escalating or getting complicated. 30 seconds on the phone saves 10 messages, one misunderstanding, and possibly one HR meeting. This signals professional judgment.
