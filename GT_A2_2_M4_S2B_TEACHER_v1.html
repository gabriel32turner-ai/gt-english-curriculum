Hot Take — Is it Confirmed?
Andrea's schedule · 60-second spoken response · Present Continuous vs might from minute one · 5 min
⏱ 5 MIN
🔥 Hot Take — Monday morning, 8 AM
Andrea just sent this message to her team: "Hi everyone — my schedule for next week is now final. I'm presenting to the board on Thursday at 10. I'm flying back Friday evening. The team might join us for the Thursday dinner — TBD. Let me know if you need anything before I leave."

What is confirmed? What is still uncertain? How does the language signal each one?
🎯 Find all Present Continuous forms → confirmed 🎯 Find all might forms → uncertain ⏱ 60 seconds spoken — no notes 🏆 Badge on timer complete
Model response (reveal after students speak)
Confirmed (Present Continuous): "I'm presenting" / "I'm flying back" — in the calendar, booked, certain.
Uncertain (might): "The team might join" — possible, not arranged yet.
Key lesson: one paragraph, two different grammar forms, two different certainty levels. Professionals use this mix every day.
60
⚡ Teacher Note — S2B Opening
S2B is full production from second one. Listen for: (1) "I will have" instead of Present Continuous — most common error, correct immediately; (2) missing contractions — "I am presenting" instead of "I'm presenting" — in spoken English, the full form sounds unnatural; (3) confusion between might (uncertain) and is/are + -ing (confirmed). Count correct Present Continuous uses — class record drives the leaderboard.

Pitch Round — Same Event, Different Certainty
Two sides · Grammar embedded in debate · 10 min
⏱ 10 MIN
💬 Setup: The company is planning a client event next month. Side A argues the event is confirmed and should be described using Present Continuous. Side B argues it's still uncertain and should use might. Both use real argument language to justify their grammar choice.
Side A — "It's confirmed — use Present Continuous"
  • The venue is booked — we're holding the event at the Marriott
  • The client confirmed attendance — they're coming on the 15th
  • The team is presenting the new product line at 3 PM
  • We're serving dinner after the presentation — catering confirmed
  • The director is opening the event — it's in her calendar
Side B — "It's still uncertain — use might"
  • The client might cancel — they haven't signed the contract yet
  • The venue might change — the Marriott may have a double booking
  • The director might not open the event — she may be travelling
  • The budget might be reduced — finance hasn't approved yet
  • The product launch might be delayed — manufacturing isn't ready
Grammar-Embedded Starters — Click to hear
We're holding the event at… The client is attending on… The director might not come because… We're presenting at 3 PM… The budget might be reduced if… Everything is confirmed — we're… Nothing is certain yet — they might…

🎯 This week at work
3 Phrases You Will Use Starting Tomorrow
PHRASE 1
"I'm meeting / We're having / She's presenting…"
Every time you write "I will have a meeting" in a WhatsApp or email this week — stop and change it to Present Continuous. One habit change = permanent grammar upgrade. Your international contacts will notice immediately.
PHRASE 2
"It's confirmed — we're [verb-ing]."
The complete professional confirmation: one colloquial phrase + one Present Continuous. "It's confirmed — we're presenting on Thursday at 10." Nothing ambiguous. Nothing uncertain. This is how senior professionals close a conversation.
PHRASE 3
"…and [person] might join — TBD."
Attach uncertainty cleanly to a confirmed schedule. "I'm having the team lunch on Friday — and Daniel might join — TBD." This shows you can manage two certainty levels in one sentence. That's real professional English.

01
Confirmed = is/are + -ing. Every time it's in the calendar, booked, arranged — Present Continuous. Not will. Not going to. This is the permanent rule.
02
The professional mix. One paragraph can have "I'm meeting" (confirmed) and "she might join" (uncertain). That mix is not confusion — it's precision. Senior English speakers do this every day.
03
Contractions are mandatory. "I'm / she's / we're" — not "I am / she is / we are" in natural spoken English. The full form sounds robotic. Contractions signal fluency.
