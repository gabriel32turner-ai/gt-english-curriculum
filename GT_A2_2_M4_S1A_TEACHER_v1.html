⚡ Teacher Note — Module 4 Opening
M4 introduces two parallel systems: may/might for possibility and Present Continuous for future for confirmed arrangements. S1A focuses entirely on may/might — the certainty spectrum. Present Continuous as a future form gets its focus in S2A. Today's core target: students leave able to distinguish "will / may / might" by degree of certainty and use may/might with bare infinitive (never "might to").

§4
Colloquial — Real Uncertainty at Work
5 natural phrases professionals use when things are not confirmed · 5 min
⏱ 5 MIN
"We'll see."
The most natural way to say "todavía no sabemos" — polite, professional, and common in all registers.
"Are you going to Guadalajara?" — "We'll see. It depends on the client."
"It depends."
Used alone or with "on": "It depends on the confirmation." Signals uncertainty without committing. Very common in meetings.
"Will you present?" — "It depends on whether the director confirms."
"I'll keep you posted."
Professional way to say "te aviso cuando sepa más." Combines uncertainty ("I don't know yet") with a commitment to update.
"We may change the schedule — I'll keep you posted."
"TBD." (To Be Determined)
Written in agendas, emails, spreadsheets. Signals an open slot. Very common in corporate English in México and internationally.
"Location: Guadalajara office (TBD — might change to video call)"
"It's in the diary."
British/international corporate English for "está confirmado en el calendario." Contrasts directly with "might" — this one is certain.
"Is the client meeting confirmed?" — "Yes, it's in the diary. Thursday at 10."
💬 Teacher Drill Note
Run a quick drill: you say a situation in Spanish, students respond with the correct colloquial phrase. Examples: "No sé si viene" → "We'll see." / "Todavía no hay fecha" → "TBD." / "Te digo cuando sepa" → "I'll keep you posted." / "Ya está en el calendario" → "It's in the diary." This builds automatic retrieval for use in real work contexts this week.
§5
Grammar — May, Might & Will: The Certainty Scale
How certain are you? · May vs Might vs Will · Bare infinitive rule · 12–15 min
⏱ 12–15 MIN
🚨 Real Work Situation
It's Monday morning. Your manager asks: "Are you coming to the Guadalajara meeting on Friday?"

You want to say: "No sé si voy — puede ser, pero no estoy segura. Depende si el cliente confirma."
❓ How do you express this in English? "I will go" sounds too certain. "I won't go" is wrong too. What do you use when you're only 30–50% sure?
✅ The Tool: May / Might / Will — Certainty Scale
Will — I will go.  →  100% certain. Already decided.
May — I may go.  →  ~50% possible. More likely than not.
Might — I might go.  →  ~30% possible. Less certain, more hesitant.
Won't — I won't go.  →  0%. Not happening.

Answer: "I might go — it depends on the client confirmation."

ModalCertaintyStructureCorporate example
Will 100% — certain Subject + will + base verb I will send the report by Friday. (It's decided.)
May ~50% — possible Subject + may + base verb The client may call this afternoon. (Probably.)
Might ~30% — less certain Subject + might + base verb She might come to the meeting. (Maybe, not sure.)
May not / Might not Negative possibility Subject + may/might not + base verb The flight might not arrive on time.
Won't 0% — certain negative Subject + won't + base verb I won't change the reservation — it's final.
⚠️ Critical error — correct immediately:
✗ "He might to come to the office." → ✅ He might come — NEVER "to" after any modal verb
✗ "She may to travel next week." → ✅ She may travel — bare infinitive always
✗ "I might to need extra time." → ✅ I might need extra time

GT Original — Probability Scale for Mexican Corporate English
  • 📊 The mental model that works: Teach students to think in percentages first — 100% / 50% / 30% / 0% — then map the modal. This makes the choice automatic.
  • ⚠️ Error: "might to" — the most common A2.2 modal error globally. Drill with a simple rule: "After ANY modal (can, will, should, may, might) → ZERO 'to'. Ever."
  • 🔊 Pronunciation: "might" /maɪt/ vs "meet" /miːt/ — two completely different vowels. "Might" rhymes with "night / right / write." Drill minimal pairs before the listening activity.
  • 💼 Corporate register: In professional emails, "may" is more formal than "might." "The meeting may be postponed" (formal email) vs "It might be postponed" (casual standup). Teach both contexts.
  • 🌍 Cultural tip: In Mexican corporate culture, people often say "sí" (meaning "maybe" or "probably") to avoid disappointing. In English, "I might" is the professional way to say the same thing — it's not rude, it's precise.

Andrea's Travel Update — Speculating About Next Week
A2.2 · M4 · S1A · Voice: Sarah (American English) · Speed: 0.82
Hi everyone — this is Andrea with a quick update on next week. Honestly, not much is confirmed yet, so let me walk you through what I'm thinking.

I might travel to Guadalajara on Thursday or Friday — it depends on the client. They may confirm by tomorrow morning, but I'm not sure yet. The flight might be full on Thursday too. If that happens, I may have to drive or take the early Friday flight instead.

For the presentation, I will definitely prepare the slides — that part is certain. But the format may change. The director might want a shorter version, or she might ask for the full Q1 data. I'll know more by Wednesday.

About the hotel — I might stay one night, or it might be two nights. It depends on whether there is a second client meeting. I may need to book something tonight just in case.

So overall, lots of "might" this week. I'll keep everyone posted as things confirm. We'll see how it develops.

Production — Your Uncertainty at Work
Real situations · May, might and will in authentic context · 12 min
⏱ 12 MIN
💬 How to run this: Students answer using may/might + bare infinitive. Partner listens and marks: (1) any "might to" error, (2) confusion between will and might. Correct immediately — this is the core accuracy goal of M4. Switch after Q3.
Question 1 — Next Week Certainty Scale
Describe 3 things from your next week at work. Use a different modal for each: one thing you WILL do (100%), one thing you MAY do (~50%), one thing you MIGHT do (~30%). Explain why each has that certainty level.
💡 Model: "I will definitely have the team meeting — it's in the calendar. I may present the Q2 results — it depends on the director. I might travel to Monterrey — we'll see."
Question 2 — What Might Go Wrong?
Think about a current project or upcoming event at work. What might go wrong or change? Use may/might to describe 3 possible problems. What will you do if each one happens?
💡 Use: "The [event] might be [delayed/cancelled/changed] because… / The [person] may not [attend/confirm/approve]…"
Question 3 — Travel Situation Role-Play
Partner A: You are a manager. Ask about your colleague's upcoming business trip — 4 questions about dates, flights, hotel, presentation. Partner B: You are Andrea. Nothing is confirmed. Answer every question using may or might. Switch roles.
💡 Manager ideas: "Will you fly or drive? / How long will you stay? / Is the hotel booked? / Will you present to the director?" Andrea: all answers use may/might.
Question 4 — Weekly Update
Imagine it's Friday at 4 PM. Your manager asks: "What's happening next week?" Give a 5-sentence spoken update — mix things that are confirmed (will) with things that are uncertain (may/might). Use at least 2 different vocabulary words from §3.
💡 Include: "I will… / I may… / It might… / We'll see… / I'll keep you posted."
Question 5 — Big Decision
You might get a promotion or change jobs in the next year. What might happen? What will you definitely do? What may change in your work life? Use all three modals — will / may / might — and explain your certainty level for each.
💡 Challenge: Use a negative too — "I might not stay in this role if… / The company may not offer…"
Question 6 — 60-Second Brief ⭐ Challenge
You are Andrea. In exactly 60 seconds, brief your manager on your travel situation: what's confirmed (will), what's possible (may), what's unlikely but possible (might), and what's not happening (won't). No notes. Partner times you.
💡 Model opener: "Here's the situation. I will definitely prepare the slides. I may fly Thursday — it depends on the client. I might need to stay two nights, but I probably won't…"

🎯 This week at work
3 Phrases You Can Use Starting Tomorrow
PHRASE 1
"I might not make it — I'll keep you posted."
Use this in your next WhatsApp group or email when you're not sure you can attend something. Natural, professional, and completely replaces "yo creo que sí voy / no sé si voy."
PHRASE 2
"The meeting may be postponed — TBD."
Write this in your next calendar invite or agenda when something isn't confirmed. "TBD" is understood globally in corporate environments and signals professionalism.
PHRASE 3
"It depends — we'll see how it develops."
The perfect answer when your foreign manager or client asks about something uncertain. Honest, confident, and natural. Never say "I don't know" alone — add "we'll see" to sound professional.

Wrap-Up — Session Checklist
3 key takeaways · I can now… · Session 2A preview · 5–8 min
⏱ 5–8 MIN
01
The certainty scale. Will (100%) → May (~50%) → Might (~30%) → Won't (0%). Think in percentages — then choose the modal. This is the mental model for all of M4.
02
Zero "to" after any modal. "She might to attend" is always wrong. Might / May + BASE verb. No exceptions — same rule as can, will, should, and must.
03
Uncertainty is professional. Saying "I might" instead of an uncertain "yes" is precise and honest in English. It's not rude — it's the correct register for corporate communication.
